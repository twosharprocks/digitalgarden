Phil English over at Toothsoup posted a [Q&A on books](https://web.archive.org/web/20130925231142/http://toothsoup.com/blog/2013/08/05/book-qa-meme/ "Q&A Meme") a few weeks back, and even though he hasn’t tagged anyone in it I thought it’d be a great way to disengage from the chaos of National Science Week and the Mars Needs Guitars shows. Needless to say there will be a full report on the shows soon (short story: they were awesome), along with an update on all the other exciting business that’s happening. But for now, here’s a bit on my reading habits!

What are you reading right now?

I actually finished reading Paulo Coelho’s “Manual of the Warrior of the Light” last night, so this morning I’ve just been reading through my regular Instapaper articles. Because of the sheer number of Space & Mars related articles I read – and how painful I find reading large amounts of text on an LCD display – I use Instapaper’s [“Read Later” applet](https://web.archive.org/web/20130925231142/http://www.instapaper.com/extras) to save large texts from websites to my Kindle reader. At midnight each night Instapaper sends my Kindle a document loaded with the previous day’s articles, and I read them with breakfast in clean & clear e-ink instead!

Do you have any idea what you’ll read when you’re done with that?

There’s my current question in life generally – what next? I’ve just finished my National Science Week performances of “Mars Needs Guitars!”, and I’m desperately trying to resolve what to do/learn/read next.

A month back I ordered a stack of generally space-related books, like a “workshop manual” for the [International Space Station](https://web.archive.org/web/20130925231142/http://www.haynes.co.uk/webapp/wcs/stores/servlet/ProductDisplay?catalogId=10001&storeId=10001&productId=55351&langId=-1), the [astronautics equivalent of Serway](https://web.archive.org/web/20130925231142/http://www.mcgraw-hill.co.uk/html/0073407755.html "Understanding Space") (the [1400-page deadly weapon](https://web.archive.org/web/20130925231142/http://www.amazon.com/Physics-Scientists-Engineers-Raymond-Serway/dp/1133947271/ref=sr_1_3?s=books&ie=UTF8&qid=1377053458&sr=1-3 "Serway") I used studying applied physics at uni), and “Apollo 13″ by Jim Lovell & Jeffery Kluger (originally published as “Lost Moon”). These are all in preparation for the [5-week summer school course in Space Science I’m doing at the University of South Australia in January](https://web.archive.org/web/20130925231142/http://www.isunet.edu/programs/southern-hemisphere-program "ISU - SHSSP"), but I also ordered a stack of classical literature and books from the “101 things to do before you die” list (Thing #52) in paperback and on Kindle.

The course on programming in Python I enrolled in has just started (and I’m ordering a Raspberry Pi to program with it), I’m researching 3D printers as I’m looking to work with Scitech to develop their 3D printing displays, a friend is about to publish her PhD thesis in political satire (I’m help edit it), and I’m keen to write-up both a book based on the “Mars Needs Guitars!” shows and another on my crazy US trip last year.

So I’m trying to work out need to find work out what is the most important to work on right now, and what to put into hibernation. But the short answer is I’ll probably re-read the Alchemist to sort my head out, then dive into a pile of Python/Raspberry Pi manuals – exciting stuff, I know.

What five books have you always wanted to read but haven’t got round to?

I’ve always wanted to read Homer’s Illiad and Odyssey – Illiad is part of the 101 Things book list, but the Greek myths have always fascinated me as they seem to reflect a strange (to me) psychology of great wisdom and knowledge married with polytheistic mysticism. The issue reading many of the ancient Greek texts comes from the archaic English most have been translated into. The version I have of The Illiad on my Kindle is the George Chapman’s 1598 translation and it’s nightmarish, but luckily I’ve found the far more modern Fitzgerald translation.

Same goes with the Bible – I’ve tried reading it a few times, but the King James bible is laborious and the Good News and NIV just felt disingenuous. So I’m looking forward to reading the [Lamsa translation when it’s published on Kindle](https://web.archive.org/web/20130925231142/http://www.amazon.co.uk/Holy-Bible-Ancient-Eastern-ebook/dp/B00BATB41E/ref=sr_1_1?s=books&ie=UTF8&qid=1377055636&sr=1-1) later in the year, as it’s being promoted as an accurate translation from the original Aramaic into modern English, without any “re-interpretation”. We’ll see.

Finally, I’ve had two books on my bookshelf unread for **far** too long. The first is “Speechreading: A Way To Improve Understanding”, which I ordered years ago to try to try to develop my audio processing. Loud noises are fine, but multiple voices in a crowded environment tend to interfere with ability to process what an individual is saying to me, so this was meant to be a way to focus my listening. The other is “The Thinking Learning Classroom” by Glenn Capelli, a school teacher and adult trainer my parents have worked with, and I’ve been meaning to reading his book since January to help engage audiences when I’m communicating science through my stand-up.

What magazines do you have in your bathroom/ lounge right now?

The short answer is I don’t – my folks leave the TV guides and Weekend magazines on the kitchen counter or in the lounge room, but my kindle follows me everywhere.

What’s the worst book you’ve ever read?

I’m going to cop a mountain of flak over this, by “Catcher In the Rye” just pissed me off all the way through. At no point did I enjoy it. I get it – it’s teenage angst, he’s finding his place in the world, whatever. And as much as I like to appear judgemental for a laugh, I’m very empathetic and generally understand where someone is coming from even if I don’t agree with the way they’re doing things. But all I could think the entire way through “Catcher In The Rye” was “if you don’t know where you’re going or what you’re doing, cool. Just stop being such a whiny little bitch about it”.

Full disclosure, I listened to the unabridged audiobook, and dozens of people have said it’s a book you need to **read** so you hear it  in your own voice in your head, rather than the audiobook narrator. Not sure it would change my opinion much, but it can’t have helped.

What book seemed really popular but you didn’t like?

As mentioned above, Catcher In The Rye

What’s the one book you always recommend to just about everyone?

“Way of the Peaceful Warrior” by Dan Millman is an easy one. Yes, it’s a self-help/spiritual novel. But it’s wisdom is clear, simple and open. Either you finish it and take it’s lessons onboard, toss it aside as crap and never pick it up again, or find yourself rereading it every year and learning something new from it (like I do). Either way, you get back from it whatever you bring to it.

Where do you usually get your books?

Since I bought a kindle late last year, I try to get everything I can as an e-book via the Amazon Kindle store. While I love being able to share books and there are **plenty** of formats Kindle does poorly (forget about text books with diagrams or graphic novels), and plenty of people complain about wanting to “feel” the book – physical books have been pretty impractical for years given the nomadic life I’ve led. In 2005 I went on a world trip with a nearly empty 80L hiking back that returned full (and over my baggage allowance) of a dozen books. I also see it as a horrendous waste – I couldn’t get “Apollo 13″ on Kindle, as I ordered it in paperback. It took 3 weeks to arrive and is composed of nearly 400 pages of paper & ink. If I’d been able to get it on Kindle I could have been reading it about 3 seconds after I paid for it, and the only environmental cost would have been the transmission of data.

When you were little, did you have any particular reading habits?

When I was in Year 7 the librarian organised a meeting with my parents because she was concerned that I wasn’t reading any fiction. I was checking out half a dozen books a week, reading them and returning them… but they were *exclusively* non-fiction. The first non-fiction book I can really remember reading an enjoying was Harry Potter and the Chamber of Secrets in first year university.

What’s the last thing you stayed up half the night reading because it was too good to put down?

Stephen King’s “On Writing”. Gets a heavy for a short part in the middle (where he starts going through a few English syntax details), but an amazing book all around. Never read any of Stephen King’s fiction, but I’m definitely adding some of his classics to the end of my (overwhelming) reading list.

Have you ever “faked” reading a book?

Not that I can remember. I’ve always been a fairly open about my preference for non-fiction, so folks are hardly surprised when I tell them I haven’t read a specific fiction text. Working through the 101 Things book list has been truly illuminating, but I’ve never felt I needed to tell someone I’d read something from the list I hadn’t – the list is a structure to broaden my reading and is for my own benefit, so there’s no motivation to convince people I’ve read more of it than I have.

Have you ever bought a book just because you liked the cover?

Again, not that I can remember. If I’m buying a book it’s generally so I can learn something, or occasionally because a few people has suggested I’d enjoy it. Reading most things on Kindle also makes the cover fairly irrelevant anyway.

What book changed your life?

The Way of the Peaceful Warrior & The Alchemist. For all my focus on non-fiction, it’s strange that two “spiritual novels” would affect me so much. But I was introduced to both just before at critical turning points in my life. Both were turning points where I made the “wrong” decision (joining the mining industry for Peaceful Warrior, joining the Marines for the Alchemist), but both books re-appeared later when I’d learnt the hard lessons already. And I read both about once a year to stop myself making the same decisions again

What is your favourite classic book?

Herman Melville’s “Moby Dick”. People hate it, and I understand why. But to me you can’t read it the way you would read any other book. It’s layered, and if you try to read it one word after another. Ask me what Moby Dick is about, and I’ll tell you it’s about an Old whaling captain going after a distinctive old sperm whale. And yet it’s so much more than that. Don’t over-analyse Moby Dick – just read without following every word and *feel* it. There were entire pages I skimmed through, then dipped in deep when I felt it pull me in.  I’ve heard it compared to listening or performing jazz – just following and weaving along towhere the muse takes the author and the readers. It’s the way Melville wrote the book, and it’s how I believe it should be read.

7 Step Parachute Landing Fall

One of the first things you’re taught when you learn to parachute is how to land without breaking a leg. If you’re learning to free fall like I did, you’re eventually taught to pull down on the control handles and “flare” your parachute at just the right height for a smooth and soft landing. But the first landing you’re taught – and the only one you can use if something goes wrong or you’re using a round parachute – is a [“PLF”, or Parachute Landing Fall](https://web.archive.org/web/20150305165153/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parachute_landing_fall).

Regardless of what others might say, a PLF still hurts… it just hurts less than trying to stay on your feet when you can’t slow down a landing. I’ve only ever had to do one: the free fall went fine, but when my parachute deployed I realised the wind was suddenly blowing at 20 knots in 180 degrees opposite to what it was when I jumped out of the plane. 180 degrees and 20 knots opposite what the jump was planned for.

I knew my parachute tracked forward at 15 knots in still air, but I was flying backwards. So I now had to choose between:

A) Turning _with_ the wind to weave among trees before hitting the ground at 35 knots (~65km/hr)  
B) Turning _against_ the wind to PLF into a hill before the trees, but hitting the ground backwards at 5 knots (~9km/hr)

I chose B: performing a PLF but still slamming backwards into a hill just meters short of the trees, and 8km off where I was _supposed_ to land. I came home with a sprained ankle and a story – choosing A could have saved my ankle or killed me.

My point is that no matter how much you prepare and plan ahead for a major event, the unexpected can and often will happen. All you can do is take a deep breath, look at the problem in front of you, and use all you know as you do your damnedest to solve it and get out alive. Once you’re through the initial impact, you bundle up your parachute, say a quick thank you to the sky and the people who trained you, then you start limping home – a little wiser than you were before.

Over the last two years I’ve been slowly building up a network of journalists, educators and science communicators to update on Mars One’s progress. It’s why I left doing stand-up in the UK and moved back to Australia – I knew I could do more as an Australian in Australia than I could as an Australian in the UK. I’ve reached out to anyone I thought would be interested all across the world, and while I’ve had fun playing a role in raising awareness about Mars One the media interest hasn’t been overwhelming.

What I didn’t expect though was the sudden jump in interest that came with being selected into the final 100 astronaut candidates two weeks ago. I sent out a press release at 2pm WAST on Monday February 16th to ~30 people, expecting replies over a few days from the usual 4 or 5. Instead I did 8 radio and TV interviews in less than 2 hours. The next day there was another 16 interviews for radio, TV and newspapers. The same again the day after. As the news broke journalists shared my details amongst each other, and the whole thing started to snowball.

That’s not even including the Today Tonight interview (can’t find the link) or the plethora of other radio interviews that happened: most assumed I lived in Melbourne or Sydney instead of Perth, so I was getting phone calls from east coast breakfast radio stations at 3am each morning saying “Sorry to call you at 6am”. It was unexpected, relentless and draining… but certainly nothing in comparison to surviving the sleep deprivation of a 5 day exercise with the Royal Marines, only to then be forced-marched through the night where I was hallucinating fences/training staff/oncoming trucks that weren’t there.

Or any other point in my time at Lympstone Commando. Like I said – you bundle up your kit, say thank you, and keep moving.

It **was** a welcome relief when the initial media rush slowed down on Wednesday afternoon though. I had a chance to embrace the end of the Perth fringe festival, support some incredible local comedian friends, see other amazing inter-state and international comedian friends, and finally retire Keith the Anger Management Koala with two incredible guest spots for “Mac the Comedy Computer” and “Tomás Ford is the End of the Fringe”.

That’s right: **Keith the Anger Management Koala is dead.**

[![keith the anger management koala](https://web.archive.org/web/20150305165153im_/http://www.themightyginge.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2015/03/keith-300x166.jpg)](https://web.archive.org/web/20150305165153/http://www.themightyginge.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2015/03/keith.jpg)

Thankfully, thankfully dead

Many of the people interviewing me asked if “being in the Mars 100 has made the mission feel more real?”. The selection really didn’t change anything about the way I see Mars One, but finishing my final performance in a furry grey koala suit did. Everything I’ve done for the last two years (and everything I continue to do) has been shaped towards helping put humans on another planet. Keith was a shield so I could share stories from the military that were still too raw to share as myself – I shared those stories on stage at the Edinburgh Fringe in 2012, and the wounds were healed. I’ve wanted to retire Keith ever since, but it wasn’t until a few months ago I made the decision to let go of the suit completely – the Mars 100 announcement just before the end of the fringe was the perfect catalyst to make that happen.

**That** felt like I’d finally closed the book on something – letting me move forward without needing to glance back. So if you want more of the koala, the suit will be available to buy in a few months when I start fundraising for my flight to Mars One’s final selection phase. I’m not ever putting it on again, but I might dry clean it if you want it.

To seal in the decision, I slipped away to Margaret River for 3 days last week. Besides seeing some incredible sunsets,

…I had a chance to think about my past, present and future, and discuss it honestly with someone I love and trust. The Mars 100 announcement has been important, but in the grand scheme it’s just a fork in the road – if I hadn’t been selected to progress to Round 3 I’d still be trying to inspire people through space exploration, and would be in the process of shifting into building spaceships instead of training to fly them.

That may still happen in the future, but for now I’ll keep flying the flag as an astronaut candidate for Mars One. Especially when it gives me a chance to talk about colonising other planets to a crowd of astronomy fans gathered to [break the Guinness World Record for “Largest Astronomy Lesson” in Kalamunda on Saturday night](https://web.archive.org/web/20150305165153/http://www.kalamunda.wa.gov.au/Events/2015/February/World-Record-Attempt-Largest-Astronomy-Lesson-2015).

The previous record was ~830 people, we had over 1100.

I have no idea what comes next. But I’ve packed up my parachute, I’m eternally grateful for the incredible and continued support through this, and we’re already moving toward the final round of selection in the second half of this year – let’s see what the rest of 2015 brings.

[![1395146_10151741469358461_449414988_n](https://web.archive.org/web/20131107175927im_/http://www.themightyginge.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2013/11/1395146_10151741469358461_449414988_n-300x273.jpg)](https://web.archive.org/web/20131107175927/http://www.themightyginge.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2013/11/1395146_10151741469358461_449414988_n.jpg)

Last week, I had no idea Mount Lawley Primary School existed. Even with all the time I’ve spent working and performing comedy around Mount Lawley through the years I’d never heard of a primary school there. And why would I? Having very little to do with school-age kids generally, I can’t imagine ever needing to know where **any** school in Mount Lawley was.

But last week Mrs H – a Year 7 teacher from Mount Lawley Primary – emailed me out of the blue. For the last 10 years she’s been throwing down a 4th term challenge to her 12 & 13 year old students: starting from scratch, how would you re-establish civilisation on another planet? And after seeing me speak on The Project and with the Nova Breakfast Crew about Mars One a few weeks back, she thought I might have something to share with her kids.

Mount Lawley Primary knows a fair bit about re-establishing from scratch already. I was in the UK at the time, and I still don’t know all the details, but it seems last year a few of their ex-students burnt the school down. Since then there’s been all sorts of complaints and politics regarding the disruption to students and the management of the school. Again, I don’t know all the details or have any opinion on how it was handled at all – I’m a comedian/physicist who’s applied to be part of the first team to colonise Mars, and have no interest in ever having kids of my own. Knowing the politics of the WA primary education system is hardly on my priority list.

But what does interest me is seeing teachers inspire kids to think beyond themselves, and to help them imagine being involved with something truly inspiring. Mrs H has been trying to do that on her own for 10 years, and now I had an opportunity to give her kids a real life example of what they were researching. She was encouraging them to think about something big, and here I am putting my hand up to help make it a reality.  
[![mars astro](https://web.archive.org/web/20131107175927im_/http://www.themightyginge.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2013/11/mars-astro-300x225.jpg)](https://web.archive.org/web/20131107175927/http://www.themightyginge.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2013/11/mars-astro.jpg)

Mrs H had given me an outline of the project her students were undertaking when she first contacted me – they had to develop a plan to re-establish civilisation on Mars, justifying the 5 people they would send, 5 animal and 10 plant species they’d take, and 10 extraneous items they’d use. But beyond that, I wasn’t sure what I was in for. Sam – one of the Year 7’s I was there to speak to – had been stationed at the front of the school to meet me, and he showed me to the visitor’s sign-in and to meet Mrs H.

Walking across the grass toward the class was a very strange experience – I wasn’t sure how I’d interact with these kids. They’re not babies, so there’d be very few technical details I’d need to simplify beyond what I’d simplified for adults previously. But would they be emotionally developed enough to wrap their heads around how big a commitment living the rest of your life on Mars is?

The moment I walked in though it got weirder – they all knew who I was. Mrs H has obviously shown them my interview on The Project, they’d done some serious research on Mars One, and they all started pouring back into the classroom from recess as soon as they saw me. Without much more than a “Hi” “This is what I did when I left school” and a “This is what I do now” from me, the questions started. And not the usual “How long does it take to get there?” or “Can you have kids?” adults ask me – these kids had some hardcore stuff to ask;

> *“How long do you think you’ll live if you get to Mars?”*
>
> *“Will you be taller in the lower gravity?”*
>
> *“What happens if your capsule is hit by a meteorite on the way there?”*
>
> *“If someone gets sick, will you put them out the airlock?”*
>
> *“Are you going to explore Mars’s moons?”*
>
> *“Why aren’t you taking a nuclear reactor?”*

And they just kept coming, question after question after question. But at no point did it feel like what they were asking was pointless or mundane – everything these 12 and 13 year old kids were asking was well researched and relevant to colonising Mars. And these kids were asking questions no one had ever asked me before. Questions that even 3 months ago I probably wouldn’t have had answers to. But today I **did** have answers for what they wanted to know, and getting answers only seemed to inspired them further.

Some of the most insightful question came from Sam, who’d met me at the gate. He was clearly excited about the prospect of having me there to query, but subdued and patient enough to wait for the opportunity to ask questions. Jody was another who was in awe but still patient enough to wait for me to ask her what her question was. Others were considerably less restrained – Jake desperately wanted to know if I could take an Xbox up with me (boredom is a very real and dangerous problem, and video games are one of many possible solutions) and if the animals we took would have their own spacesuits (the first animals we might take would be insects for their high protein and low maintenance, and they wouldn’t need suits living in the pressurised habitats). Sarah suddenly lit up like a Christmas tree when I explained I used to work with explosives, pouring out dozens of questions that ranged from group psychology to storing food to landing procedures.

Two of the more interesting questioners seemed keen to show off their own knowledge. Tim wanted to know if Mars One was investigating Phobos & Deimos, Mars’s Moons – they aren’t, the initial mission is entirely focussed on colonising Mars. But the question served as an amazing opportunity to explain that in space, distance doesn’t always equal more difficult: the amount of fuel required to send a robotic sample-return mission to Phobos is actually less than it is to go to the Moon, because even though it’s much further away Phobos has far less gravity to contend with. Joel threw me though – why we weren’t taking a fusion reactor to Mars with us. Never in my life did I imagine having to explain the nuclear physics of break-even fusion reactors to justify an architecture decision on a manned mission to Mars… to a slightly awkward, softly spoken 12 year old.

We had around 30 kids in the class though, and I’d heard from 6. It wasn’t until I started ignoring their hands and started asking along the rows if they had a question that the rest opened up. I’m still in shock at the quality of the questions these kids asked though. None of them were mean-spirited, none of them thought it was a stupid idea, and none of them thought Mars One would fail – they simply wanted to know how we would do it, and what challenges would be faced getting there. These scary smart kids had processed the enormity of the project, understood why it was worth the risk, and recognised that by the time some of them finished university the first humans would be living permanently on another planet.

90 minutes later the bell for lunch rang, and the kids wouldn’t leave. Even long after everyone else had been finally shooed out, Jody waited to ask me who I would want coming with me to Mars. I told her about the three friends I featured in my national science week show “Mars Needs Guitars” – Sian, Paul and Eliane – and explained how none of them could come with me, but each had a personality type that would play an important role in a team heading to Mars.

After Jody left with her answer, the teachers at Mount Lawley Primary had one more student to introduce me to: Ryan. I never asked how old he is, but since he wasn’t in the class I’d spoken to I assumed he’s a Year 6, making him 11 or 12. The first thing you notice about Ryan is the motorised wheelchair – he’s got fairly major muscular dystrophy, with only limited control over his limbs. This isn’t a big deal, but I’d be lying if the massive black wheelchair around him wasn’t the first thing I noticed looking at him.

The next thing you’d notice about Ryan is he knows more about science than anyone under 18 I’ve ever met. **Ever**. The teacher caring for him joked saying Ryan made up for not being able to run around by being able to learn everything. I thought she was just being kind to him. I was wrong. It’s one thing to be a little surprised by a question on the construction of an inflatable habitat coming from a class of year 7’s that are specifically studying how to colonise Mars. It’s something else altogether to be discussing relative Mars habitat pressure, gyroscope and retrorocket control in the [SpaceX “Grasshopper”](https://web.archive.org/web/20131107175927/http://www.space.com/23193-spacex-grasshopper-rocket-highest-hop-video.html), then calculating the average speed in kilometres per second of a Earth-Mars transit during a Hoffman transfer… with a kid who’s at primary school, who’d struggle to turn the pages of a book by himself, and who’s only been learning about space **in his spare time outside of school**.  
[![boxer](https://web.archive.org/web/20131107175927im_/http://www.themightyginge.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2013/11/boxer.jpg)](https://web.archive.org/web/20131107175927/http://www.themightyginge.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2013/11/boxer.jpg)

And the third thing you notice about Ryan? He’s one of the most humble human beings I’ve ever met. Unlike Joel who asked earlier about nuclear fusion to partly show everyone he knew what it was, Ryan simply asked if all the launch stages would be chemical rockets. I explained that currently Mars One is planning to use heavy-lift chemical rockets to low Earth orbit, then use more chemical rockets to propel the transit hab to Mars. So the next questions were about the fuelled weight and lift capacity of two heavy lift chemical rockets – SpaceX’s Falcon 9 and ArianeSpace’s Ariane 5. We didn’t need to discuss ion drives, nuclear rockets or any other exotic type of propulsion so he could show me how much he knew about them – he just wanted to know what type of rockets we were using so he could learn more about Mars One.

Talking to Ryan though, it became very clear his main interest in Mars One is how we’ll *get* there – the propulsion system, orbital transfer and landing systems. And while I’m learning as much as I can about the specifics of rocket science, I’m also trying to learn about all the **other** incredibly diverse aspects of the Mars One mission at the same time. Things like low energy hydroponic horticulture, astrobiology, interplanetary communication systems and long-term isolation psychology to name only a fraction.

Inevitably, this amazing primary school kid threw me a question I didn’t have an answer to: what average speed will the capsule be travelling at during the transit from Earth to Mars? I knew it’d be fast, but I didn’t have even the faintest idea on a figure. So we worked it out. And by “we”, I mean I grabbed a calculator and **he** worked it out (correctly) at around 2.7 km per second. Mostly in his head.

When I was in Year 6 Andy Thomas became Australia’s first professional astronaut, launching into space on STS-77 in May 1996. But I barely remember it. Without going into too many details, that was the year I clashed with a teacher in a fairly ugly year-long saga of her claiming to my parents I was mentally disabled and ostracising me from the rest of the class. It only ended after an incident that lead to her taking several months psych leave, and me telling my Mum it would have been “so much easier to just step in front of the school bus than face (my teacher) again”.

I don’t resent my Year 6 teacher – it happened, I moved on to Year 7, then high school, then the rest of my life started happening. But I know it disrupted my love of science and changed how I saw myself for a long time. Getting the second highest score in the 1997 UNSW National Science Competition helped put me back on the right track. But I also know had my Year 7 teacher given my class the challenge of working out how they would colonise another planet, and then got someone in who had dedicated their life to do it… my life so far would have been very different. Or had Andy Thomas visited my Year 7 class, I’d have made some **very** different choices. Not better, not worse – I wouldn’t change any of the experiences I’ve had, even the near-death ones. But I would have walked a different path through high school and beyond.

The kids at Mount Lawley Primary didn’t deserve to have their school burn down last year, any more than I deserved to be treated the way I was in Year 6. And every day I read and hear streams of [cynicism](https://web.archive.org/web/20131107175927/http://onlineathens.com/opinion/2013-11-04/yarbrough-one-way-trip-mars-seems-wrong-move) and [bitterness](https://web.archive.org/web/20131107175927/http://www.news.com.au/technology/science/saudi-sheikh-ali-al-hemki-declares-fatwa-against-manned-missions-to-mars/story-fnjwlcze-1226750128434) about what Mars One is trying to achieve, and it hurts because it reads like “You’re **stupid** for even trying” or “You’ll **never** be smart enough to do this”. But it’s not coming from those 12 & 13 year olds – they know it’s worthy, possible and we’re more than capable of making it happen.

So if I can help inspire a class of kids by simply talking to them about what Mars One are doing, then I think together we’ve already helped make the world a better place. Regardless of the nay-sayers I’ll keep talking to those kids about Mars One and what I’m doing, because I know what a difference having someone do it 17 years ago would have made to me.

And maybe in another 17 years time one of them will be able to talk to me about it in person on Mars, arriving in a rocket designed by Ryan.

When I was 9, I went on my first scientific exploration. My goal? To explore the dangerous and uncharted territory of the abandoned block across the road from our house, in search of Velociraptors. Just like the great expeditions of Edmund Hillary climbing Everest or Neil Armstrong setting foot on the Moon, my expedition required extensive planning (15 minutes deciding which dirt mounds to search behind first), emergency supplies to survive unexpected contingencies (a sandwich and juice-box in my schoolbag), and a dedicated support team (my 6-year-old sister watching from our house).

Before setting out on this epic expedition, I’d done my best to survey the block from a distance. Peering through my toy binoculars with their cutting edge 2x magnification power, I was fairly sure there were no large wild animals living among the piles of rubbish along the block’s back fence. But I couldn’t be sure a pack of Velociraptors weren’t lying in wait. Jurassic Park had just been in cinemas, dinosaur fever was at full strength, and exploring on foot was the only way to be certain that a raptor pack hadn’t survived unnoticed for 71 million years in the unused suburban house block across the street.

So I packed my lunch, hugged my sister, and bravely ventured forth into the unknown. In what is surely disappointing news for dinosaur lovers everywhere, I didn’t discover any Velociraptors that day. Or any other dinosaurs. And I didn’t find any the following day when I went back to check again. I didn’t even find evidence of dinosaurs – no nests, no egg fragments, no giant bird-like footprints. Turns out it really was just another empty house block, one that would have a new house built on it a few months later. But until I explored it myself, mapped the dirt piles on my clipboard with marker pens, and checked then rechecked the bushes and rubbish, I wouldn’t be satisfied.

It might have been an inauspicious and slightly misguided start, but that dinosaur hunt fired the natural passion I believe we all have to explore and learn more about the universe we live in. I was discovering that while we’re all scared of the unknown, by exploring and asking questions for ourselves the unknown eventually becomes known. I wasn’t just exploring an empty house block – I was also discovering who I was.

These days my expedition plans are taking me a little further afield: I’ve been selected as one of 43 Australian candidates for the Mars One mission to land the first humans on Mars in 2025. Over 200,000 people applied worldwide in 2013, with 1058 potential candidates selected for testing and interviews. It’s still a long way to Mars though, with the 1058 of us being narrowed down to between 24 and 40 by June 2015 to begin 9 years of training. The crew selected to make the historic first mission will be composed of just 4 people.

And they won’t be coming back. This is a one-way colonisation mission.

While we’ve had the technology to go to Mars since the 1980′s, the greatest engineering and financial challenges of a mission to Mars are linked to having the fuel and launch systems to return from Mars to Earth. So Mars One have cut out the return trip: every 2 years after the first crew lands, another crew of 4 will arrive, slowly building up the colony. The colonists will land, make Mars their home and live out the rest of their lives there. The first crews will simply be keeping the colony functioning – learning to grow their own food, maintaining life support systems, sweeping dust off the solar panels that will power the habitat. Just surviving will be ground-breaking exploration. But over time we’ll venture out further to study Mars itself – studying it’s geology and dust storms, searching for water… and maybe life.

You’re still probably asking the same question countless others have asked me already: Why would ANYONE sign up for this? Why would anyone leave their friends and family behind, with no hope of return, to live and die on a cold, dead planet orbiting 350 million kilometres away? Mars is so far away you can’t even phone people back on Earth – it takes light between 3 and 20 minutes to travel between the two planets, and that’s when the Sun isn’t blocking communication entirely. The first crew will spend 7 months locked inside a small transit habitat, hurtling through the darkness of space and eating pre-packaged food, perform the first atmospheric entry of Mars with humans on-board, and land on another planet to live out the rest of their lives inside a habitat, because the atmosphere on Mars will kill you only fractionally less quickly than open space will.

Why would anyone take the risk? Why wouldn’t you just stay in the comfort of Earth? The simple answer is that staying on Earth is no better than surveying that abandoned house block from the safety of our front yard through toy binoculars. We are all scientists and explorers at heart, it’s just that many of us have learnt to fear what we don’t know instead of taking the risk to explore to find answers.

To learn more about our universe, we need to ask questions, we need to explore, and we need to take calculated risks to carry out that exploration. The history of science is filled with scientific explorers who asked questions and took risks in the pursuit of a fuller understanding of our universe. In an era where Polio was as feared as nuclear war, Jonas Salk conducted the first human vaccine trial on himself and his family. When calculation by sliderule was the norm, Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin kept cool through multiple computer alarms and low fuel warnings to be the first to land on the moon. Galileo Galilei discovered Europa, Calisto, Io and Ganymede orbiting Jupiter, then faced the Roman Inquisition for his outspoken support for the ludicrous idea that the Earth orbits the Sun – an idea we take for granted 400 years later, but a dangerous idea for the time.

Science and exploration are about expanding our comfort zone; discovering more about the universe and ourselves; and then sharing what we’ve learnt with others. We explore and ask questions so that others can ask more questions and conduct further expeditions, and so we can continue to develop and evolve as a species. This in turn all helps to make our little patch of the universe a better place to live in.

I don’t know if I’ll be part of the first crew on Mars – the road there is long and dangerous, and there are many others who seek the same goal who may be better suited to it than I am. But I know that we must. That we, as a species, have not left low-Earth orbit since Apollo 17 returned from the Moon in 1972 is a tragedy. But to stop asking questions and to stop exploring would be a far greater one. The technology and science we take for granted today was hard-won by scientists and explorers – all driven by that eternal question of “why?”, and brave enough to try to find out.

I’m certain that humans will colonise Mars, reach out into the rest of the solar system, and eventually the rest of the universe. But only if we as a species keep exploring and keep asking questions. And I’ll admit the chances of finding Velociraptors on Mars is pretty slim, but who knows? Maybe we’ll find something else that can teach us where life came from, and how it fits into the universe. We need only ask and explore.

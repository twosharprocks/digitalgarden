During your walk this morning you finished "Surely Your Joking Mr Feynman", and it's been an interesting audiobook to listen to. 

When I first started it, I was utterly convinced that it was absolutely brilliant - that Feynman was really connecting with that passion for the universe that I crave, that he had plenty of character and quirks that made him an engaging personality who genuinely loved learning about the universe. I'm sure that's all true, but the further into the book I got the less I liked him - the whole thing started to feel like a collection of little stories from his life that didn't build or develop into anything. Just snippets of experience and fun "cocktail event" type stories.

Thinking about it now, there was no true vulnerability in the book - at least not much of it. There's no doubt that Feynman went through hell with the death of his first wife, and I certainly understand that not being vulnerable was the culture of the time he grew up through, but it certainly feels like less of a book as a result. The more I reflect on it, the more I realise that it felt hollow at the end - like he'd run out of light and amusing anecdotes, so the book just ended.

The way that he campaigned for science education and shared the wonders of physics is extraordinary, and it absolutely should be celebrated. But the book itself felt like it lacked blood - like we were seeing a mask of Richard Feynman, and not getting to know the living, breathing, feeling human being behind it. Maybe that's the way he was in real life too - masking with humour, pranks and flirting. There's no doubt he was an extraordinary educator, and that his work was certainly worthy of a Nobel prize. 

But this book - the most celebrated and well-known book he wrote - feels a little like "Becoming Martian" feels to me now: a first book that lacks blood and tears. It's easy, light reading, and that's important. But I think it's also vital to genuinely bare your soul when you write, and to tell those hard to acknowledge truths about yourself. People read kind-of-autobiographies like "Surely You're Joking, Mr Feynman" so that they can connect with and understand the author better, but this book feels like the reader is being kept at arms length. 

There's the perception of sharing and wonder, but it doesn't feel entirely genuine and certainly doesn't feel raw. That's perfectly okay for a Nobel prize-winning, but it's a something I want to keep in mind for my own writing - always make sure you're being honest with your readers, and never hesitate to overshare and bleed on the page, because fearless vulnerability will set you free.

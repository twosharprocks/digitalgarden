# Additional Notes

It is a complex underground network dug into Middle Triassic limestone, totaling 3865 m of galleries and shafts, achieving a level difference of 219 m.

The mouth of the pit, located at 1166 m altitude, has an oval shape of 6/3 m. Right from the mouth begins the sequence of shafts, the descent rope is tied to the nearest fir tree. The first 10 m jumper is partially clogged with tree trunks that get in the way when going down but help when going up. At the base of the first shaft there is a sloping room. The rope is tied to a log caught between the walls. The vertical has a small terrace at 3 m then falls 8 m into a room full of boulders, Doru Hall. On the way back, a good climber can avoid this vertical by climbing the wall on the left and then crossing to the intermediate step.

From Doru Hall, the rope is attached to a piton planted in the rock and the 10 m. descent is made into the next hall. A trickle of water already appears, which wets the speleologist during the descent. Immediately follows the 11 m vertical where the rope is tied to the piton as well as the next 8 m. After the last jumper, follows Gaura de Soarece 1, at an elevation of -70 m from the mouth of the abyss. This is a gallery of less than 1 m in diameter on which the water draft is blocked by, ironically, a metal gate, the Egoist Gate, installed by the speleologists from Sfinx in Gârda de Sus .

After the gate you reach the edge of a 14 m long drop which is actually in the ceiling of a gallery. You land in another descent. Upstream the Red Gallery clogged with clay closes after a few hundred meters. Downstream you descend the spiral gallery which turns into Mouse Hole 2, very dirty and wet. You can avoid it by taking a drop which takes you directly to the Confluence Hall. From the left comes the Floor Gallery, then another from the right, the Rain Gallery. From here you reach the Great Hall.

The Floor Gallery, about 360 m long, is ascending, with a lot of alluvium and rubble on which calcite floors have formed. A stream of water comes from the gallery and stagnates in a small lake just below the window behind Gaura de Şoarece 2.

The Rain Gallery, which heads north, is active, with a stream flowing towards the Confluence Hall. It is high and sinuous, more subfossil in nature, and branches into the Serban Gallery from a fold on the left, then the Alluvial Gallery also on the left as we advance, and the Twisted Gallery on the right. Both galleries are active, ascending, and have the characteristic that the ceiling and part of the walls are made up of breccias [ 2 ] of calcareous elements trapped in a matrix of red clay.

The downstream sector begins with the Confluence Hall, which gradually widens into the Great Hall, impressive in size, horizontality and the smoothness of the walls, which are fault faces [ 3 ] . This hall is crossed by a stream that disappears into a sinkhole that may form a lake. To the south, the hall gradually narrows, transforming into a gallery 5—6 m wide, full of boulders. The floor suddenly disappears into a 30 m deep chasm divided in half by a boulder caught between the walls. Down below, one reaches an impressive hall, dominated by the high cliff, above which the end of the Great Hall can be glimpsed.

Here comes from the right (from an impenetrable gallery) a water that gathers all the streams encountered above. The flow, now considerable, has created a typical active gallery, with marmites [ 4 ] , spoons [ 5 ] and polished walls. After two smaller waterfalls, the water flows to the right into the Siphon Gallery which, after the first 13 m high step and then a second 5 m, becomes horizontal and ends in a siphon lake at an elevation of -168.

Before the 13 m waterfall, which falls to the right, the main gallery presents a strong positive unevenness, the Czech Threshold, which posed great problems to the first explorers. Reaching the top of the threshold, we realized that we were again under the ceiling of the Great Hall, at the same level as its floor. At this distance, the stream found another flow path, leaving the Czech gallery suspended, and forming an anti-tectural staircase [ 6 ]

Once its secrets have been discovered, the Czech Threshold is relatively easy to overcome. The Czech Gallery that follows it presents narrow sections and large halls, such as the Pagoda Hall, richly decorated with draperies, or the Crash Hall, which represents the largest gap in the cave (80 m long, 30 wide and 30 high). A 9 m shaft opens from the floor of the last hall that descends to the open. A fossil gallery full of floor septa follows [ 7 ] . which ends on the edge of another 12 m shaft. If you can climb to the right over a saddle and reach a wall in the slope that leads to the Found River Hall. From the right comes a river that can be followed upstream to a terminal siphon. Downstream from the point where the water met again, the gallery, narrow and meandering, although active, presents beautiful decorations: stalactites, stalagmites, columns, white montmilch leaks [ 8 ] that cannot be destroyed by floods, the river flow being regulated by the siphon [ 9 ] upstream. After another disappearance of the water, with a detour on an upper gallery and its reappearance, the gallery ends in a sand siphon. Correlation with surface formations shows that this point is only a few dozen meters from the water's outlet to the surface in Izbucul Poliței.

The Şesuri Cave is one of the most interesting caves in the Apuseni Mountains due to the variation of its aspects and its complex genesis. It presents all types of galleries (pressure, joints with levels and terraces, with friction mirrors), and the unevenness clearly highlights the stages of formation.

The pothole has been known for hundreds of years by locals from the hamlets of Ghețar and Ocoale. In May 1950, the first exploration took place by Dan Coman and Mihai Șerban, supported by five climbers from Cluj. A team of climbers from the Central Army Headquarters in Brașov also joined the explorations, which continued until 1955. In 1951, the depth of -180 m (in reality -167 m) was reached, with these figures being the national record for unevenness until 1955. In keeping with the communist regime, the pothole was named "1 Mai", later it reverted to its local name.
In 1971-1972, a team (almost militarily organized) of Czechoslovak speleologists resumed the exploration of the Avenul din Sesuri, installing underground camps, a telephone line to the surface, etc. In these expeditions, the continuation is discovered and the terminal siphon is reached at -220 m. J. Hromas, J. Hýsek and P. Nosek perform a relatively precise topography for 2,500 m of galleries.
In March 1978 Liviu Vălenaș and Nicolae Sasu manage to traverse the entire cavity without any underground camp. In this tour, organized for the first time in the TSA style, a fossil floor above the terminal siphon and several side and lower floors are discovered. In April 1978, a joint camp of the "Z" and "Speodava" caver clubs is organized (Liviu Vălenaș, Petru Brijan, Nicolae Sasu, Ovidiu Cuc and Eva Györfi). On this occasion, the upper floor starting above the terminal siphon is surveyed and the Planșee, Roșie and Alluvium galleries are completely explored and precisely surveyed. The most spectacular advance, however, was made on the main active gallery (Galeria Întortocheată), where, by climbing a 3 m waterfall, the Czechoslovak team's terminus from 1971-1972 was far exceeded. Liviu Vălenaș and Petru Brijan, after overcoming a succession of waterfalls, the highest being 7 m, and by passing several open siphons, reached within a few meters of the Vuiuga ponor, the network's insurgency. Finally, in the Access Gallery area, at -70 m, by descending a narrow shaft, ignored by the Czechoslovakians, a labyrinthine floor is discovered, explored and surveyed, with low and narrow galleries, with a total length of 166 m. In July 1978, Liviu Vălenaș, Nicolae Sasu and a team of Polish cavers from the Dabrowa Górnicza club undertake other explorations in the Avenul din Sesuri, reaching the current length of 4,270 m.
After 1978, nothing more was discovered in this chasm, but various impostors claimed galleries as having been discovered by them, in reality they were the galleries explored and surveyed by Liviu Vălenaș, Petru Brijan, Nicolae Sasu, Ovidiu Cuc and Eva Györfi in March-April 1978.

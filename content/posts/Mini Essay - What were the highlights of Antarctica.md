Today marks two months since the end of [Trip - Antarctica - 2025 Nov]({{< relref "posts/Trip - Antarctica - 2025 Nov.md" >}}), and I feel like it's well overdue for a reflection on what happened and the highlights of visiting the frozen continent. 

The most immediate aspect of visiting Antarctica is that it was my "7th continent". I still don't feel like I've properly visited South America, as I really just flew through Santiago and Buenos Aires on the way to Ushuaia, then wandered around Ushuaia for a few days before boarding the ship to Antarctica... but I have now set foot on all 7 continents. That was never really that big a deal for me though - I never set out to visit all 7 continents (even though it's one of the [Bucket List]({{< relref "posts/Bucket List.md" >}})) and I've always been a little disgusted by folks who count the number of countries they've visited. Travel is extraordinary and vital to our emotional growth, but visiting or ticking off places for just the sake of it always felt disrespectful to the places you were visiting. I've travelled extensively through North America and Europe, done multiple visits to Africa, and been to a variety of places through Asia too. But this most recent trip is the only time I've been to South America, and it felt like it was just a trip in transit to Antarctica. Visiting Antarctica is understandably difficult so a 10 day trip is not to be ignored, but I still don't feel like I've really experienced the continent either - that's something I think will require an overwinter stay. So ticking off my 6th and 7th continents weren't really highlights from the trip.



Seeing orca as we arrived at Deception Island was one of the most unexpected and genuinely incredible things I've ever seen. To have our ship surrounded by three separate pods, with 40-45 animals swimming on every side of the ship, was simply breath-taking. I just remember locking eyes with one of the expedition guides and seeing the seer awe in her face at what we were witnessing, and realising this was as special for the people guiding us as it was for the folks who'd paid to be there. It was here that part of me wished I had a substantial digital camera, but I also know that I simply loved being in a place where I could enjoy the spectacle of the animals rather than trying to capture it. Seeing such stunning animals breaching with their young was extraordinary, and I still don't really have words for it. 

Setting foot on deception Island didn't really mean much, and if I'm honest it was all a bit uncomfortable - I was more worried about Stacey, Soori, and Jeanette all being unwell, although I wasn't overly impressed with Deception Island if I'm honest. It also didn't count as "setting foot on Antarctica" either, as it wasn't part of the peninsula. Kissing the rock while kayaking with Jeanette meant a lot more though, mostly because of how ridiculous and fun it felt. It was also in the larger context of the extraordinary whale experience we'd had that morning, and the 2nd humpback experience we had a little later on. The time that I really "set foot on Antarctica" was the polar plunge, which was a lot of fun but not really an opportunity to soak up the experience of "touching" the continent... even if I was barefoot.

As fun as it was, the polar plunge was really overshadowed by kayaking all week to see a leopard seal. I still get emotional thinking about what an incredible experience that was, and how utterly life-changing it was to see such an awe-inspiring animal up close and in a genuinely peaceful state. I'd gone to Antarctica with the hope of seeing a leopard seal, but had never been brave enough to truly admit that was my reason for being there. My 5 star review of "Keith looks back in Anger" came out the day I cancelled the show, but I never felt bad about it because I knew the show had done exactly what it needed to do... and I kind of dismiss all the other things that happened during this trip when I think about how important it was to kayak next to that leopard seal. How life-changing it was to spend a week on the water silently hoping for an encounter, and then being able to experience a half ton apex predator sleeping on an iceberg with just one other kayak on the water. I felt like spending time around that leopard seal was a way of honouring the 24 year old kid who was leaving the Royal Marines and didn't know what he wanted to do with his life, and who saw a photo on a doctor's wall of a kayak and a leopard seal in Antarctica and said "I'm going to do that one day". I'm not the same person who sat in that doctor's office 15 years ago, but I'm very grateful to him that he saw something extraordinary on the wall and decided to create the opportunities to experience it himself.

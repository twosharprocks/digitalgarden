In early January 2010, I was on Dartmoor doing a survival exercise with the Royal Marine Commandos. I'd been training with them for a few months, and somewhere between the days of trudging through 6ft of soft snow and shivering through the -20°C nights, I was bitten by a deer tick. I didn't know at the time that I'd been infected with Lymes disease, but 5 weeks later I was admitted to CTCRM's hospital where I lost 8 kg in 5 days and nearly died.  
  
I spent the next 10 weeks in the Hunter Company rehabilitation unit; learning to walk, then run, climb, and eventually yomp with a rifle and pack again. When I returned to training I was fitter than I'd ever been, but a week later I heard a recruit say he'd kill unarmed prisoners if he had the chance, and I knew after all I'd seen and done that I couldn't be part of the military any more.  
  
During my discharge process I had to be medically cleared by a doctor, and as I sat waiting for her to sign my records I spotted a photo on the office wall of her kayaking in Antarctica with a leopard seal. I asked her about it, and after a few jokes about eating penguins I said "I'm going to do that one day". It's been over 15 years since I sat in that doctor's office, but on the 23rd of November 2025 I did exactly what I said I'd do - I kayaked with a leopard seal in Antarctica.  
  
With all the bullshit of "normal" life, it's easy to lose sight of the fact that we're so much more powerful and capable than the world might have us believe. We're capable of doing ridiculous and extraordinary things if we want to, and given the shit I've survived it's been surprisingly easy to forget just how lucky I am to be here at all. So when an email from Oceanwide Expeditions arrived in early October to join an expedition to Antarctica just 5 weeks later, I booked my flights to Ushuaia that same hour. When I was offered the choice between landing on the Antarctic Peninsula to see penguins up close, or paddling off-shore in a kayak, I chose to spend the entire expedition kayaking because I knew it'd provide the best chance of seeing a leopard seal. Because that was my best opportunity to do something I'd dreamed about for more than 15 years.  
  
Some dates stick with you forever. 19 April 2008 was the night I decided to keep living. 14 December 2009 was the night I discovered I'm more scared of not being strong enough to help someone in trouble than I am of dying myself. 10 June 2010 was the day I left a career that would ask me to harm others. 7 September 2012 was the day I knew I wanted to die on another planet, and 16 February 2015 was the day I could finally tell people I'd been shortlisted with 99 others to do it.  
  
There are a lot of other dates, but I know I'll remember November 23rd as one of the most important ones. Not because I saw an apex predator up close for the first time. Not because it was the peak experience of a generally life-changing trip. And not because I jumped into sub-zero water immediately afterwards wearing just a pair of budgie-smugglers wedged up my butt-crack.  
  
I'll remember November 23rd because it's the day I kept a 15 year promise to my younger self - a scared, 25 year old kid who was lost, hurt, and grasping for meaning as he left a career he thought he'd been born for. Seeing that leopard seal was a way of keeping a promise to him that I'd do extraordinary things with my life, and that I wouldn't have to harm anyone in the process.  
  
I'm eternally grateful to my Antarctic "Cheesies" - Stacey, Soori, Jeanette, Beth - for being so incredibly open, honest, vulnerable, and supportive throughout such a life-changing trip. You each make the world better by being in it, and I'm so glad I got to share November 23 (and the other 9 days of our expedition) with each of you. I'm also supremely thankful to Nick, Alexis, Vix, Chloe, and the rest of the Oceanwide Expeditions team. Seeing each of you doing what you love - and excitedly sharing it with others in one of the most extraordinary places on Earth - reminded me that every morning I have the choice to wake up and be excited to do what I love, and to be damn good at it.  
  
A friend underwent some life-changing surgery this year, and said a few days ago that "2026 needs to watch out though… I’ve got lost ground to make up for". I think she's right. For me it's been way too easy to let dreams rot, to blame that numbness on being tired from work, to assume that tomorrow will be the same as yesterday, and to keep doing things because it's easier to fit the demands of others instead of being honest about what makes us love being alive. Because I do love being alive, and this expedition to one of the most incredible places on the planet has reminded me that I don't need to hide it.  
  
So 2026 better watch out - I don't know what it'll bring, but I'm excited to figure out the next adventure... and I don't plan on taking another 15 years to complete it.

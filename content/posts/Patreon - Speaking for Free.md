Over the last two years I've had a couple of fairly nasty experiences with friends and colleagues asking me to speak at their STEM event.

My general rule for the last few year has been to charge everyone booking me to speak, even if it's just a small amount, because as soon as money is involved everyone (schools in particular) take things much more seriously. When they're paying for your time, people make sure the presentation room is ready for you, the projector is working, and the kids are prepped. Teachers get their shit together when something is coming out of their budget, and because they're so focused on doing their jobs they also tend to dismiss anything they're getting for free. Zero dollars, zero investment.

Where this breaks down though is when I try to do a favour for a friend. The usual pattern has been someone I've known for years and often worked asks me to talk about Mars One at their event (a festival, school-opening, etc), which I agreed to do for free because I want to support someone who's science communication efforts I value. Once I'm in touch with their superiors to work out the details though, the superiors immediately see as a free resource to exploit and start throwing other things into the mix that I didn't agree to. Things I wouldn't have agreed to do for free if they'd been discussed upfront, but I will now feel bad turning down because I initially agreed to support a friend.

The worst of these situations involved the opening of a huge technology education centre that had numerous state and federal ministers present for the ribbon cutting ceremony because of the millions of dollars of government and private sector money that had been invested into it... Not enough to pay for a keynote speaker though apparently. I was initially invited to be there in presence only, but a few days before the event my participation was suddenly bumped up to delivering the 10 minute keynote address that I'd otherwise charge quite a bit of money for.

Nevertheless I spent a few days developing a tailor-made keynote to talk about the importance of STEM and centres like this one, turned up on time, saw my name printed on all the event brouchures marked "Keynote address" with when I'd be speaking just after the state education minister and before a student group presented an interpretive dance. But when the minister finished, the students were invited to the stage without mention of me. I was still standing there with my notes waiting on the side of the stage ready to be introduced when the education minister was invited back on, cut the ribbon with a robot, and the MC thanked everyone for coming...

Turns out they'd run over time, so they decided that I - the unpaid, unelected outsider who wasn't a student - would be the one who would be cut from the event because of their poor event management, but didn't even tell me beforehand, and didn't come up to me afterwards to explain. They simply forgot I was there at all.

If I sound angry it, well I am. There were all sorts of apologies and claims made to "make it up to me", but a year later I've heard nothing and I suspect the entire thing is ancient history to them. For me though it was in important reminder not to ever agree to anything I don't want to do, that I'm a professional speaker and author who should be paid for my time, and renegotiate when situations change.

I mention all of this because I'm concerned that I've half made the same mistake again for an upcoming event - agreeing to a fantastic series of keynotes that I am being paid for, but that has had a "sponsors event" tacked on that I'm now speaking at too. I was given the option of increasing my fee to include it, and it is very low-key... but still the concern remains that I'll be left standing on the side of the stage holding my notes when a minister speaks for too long. That I'll be cut because I'm not providing them any money or charging them anything.

Before the event last year at the the technology centre I wrote up my keynote in full, and after being left unexpectedly hanging I had planned to share it here on Patreon but never did. With this similarly themed event coming up very soon, I've decided to write and share this keynote on why STEM is so important before I deliver it. It'll help me shape my thoughts ahead of the event itself, give you an insight on the sort of things I share at keynote addresses, and I'll have a sense of relief that if I do experience something similar to what happened last year at least my work will still be read.

With all of that out of the way, here's the keynote I'll be delivering on why STEM is so important to me and why speaking to students is a critical component in that. I've cut out the thank you's and acknowledgements to sponsors that start and end the keynote I'll actually deliver, and we're picking up from the start of the interesting bits: my personal STEM story.

Enjoy!

----------------------------------------------------

I was not a good science student in school. At all. There was no question that I understood what was being taught, or that I like science - I just didn't like the science that was being taught. I was one of those nightmare kids that teachers know "how far he could go if he just applied himself". But I was bored. The science we were being taught was boring, intended to meet a curriculum that no student had a say in, and that I couldn't see how it could ever be useful.

Put simply, I was a trouble-making smartass.

I studied enough to pass, but what I really cared about was trying things myself and doing. I didn't learn about Hooke's constant in a classroom - I learned about it when we built a zipline in the backyard and the rope kept stretching so much we'd slam into the ground on each run. I learned about plastic deformation when we tension-ed the rope up so much it was a meter longer when we returned it to my Dad's shed, hoping he wouldn't notice. He did. I also learned about yield points when we hooked it up a week later and the rope snapped the moment I leaped off out of the treehouse onto it.

When I wasn't learning about science and engineering by doing things that could kill me, I was learning about science and engineering by making things that could kill me. My high school' infamous "Cubbyhouse Wars" of 1999 came to an abrupt end when the smokebombs one group made were used to burn down my group's spike-covered cubbyhouse fortress, scorching the crossbow I'd carefully designed and built to fire high-powered gumnuts. No one was every going to teach me to make a gumnut crossbow or a smokebomb, but I developed a sense for engineering design and studied chemistry to work them out.

I wanted to do interesting and fun things like discover dinosaurs or build robots and rockets to space. I hated high school chemistry but explosions, so I sat through three years of boring classes so I could make things that you can wind up on a watchlist for without losing any of my fingers in the process. It's probably not a surprise that the Army or the British Commandos wanted me to explore that further. But deep down I've just always wanted to know how things work and discover more about the universe we live in... but the universe feels pretty lifeless when you're staring at a whiteboard or an exam paper.

Math has always felt like my weakest point of STEM, something I'm sure many of us feel. Yet it's applied math that saved my life when a parachute jump went wrong in 2006 - knowing my parachute's forward speed versus the wind that had suddenly turned around 180 degrees told me what speed I'd be hitting the ground and if it'd break my legs, and my current altitude versus my descent rate told me whether to try to steer over the looming trees or try to crash land in front of them. Math told me to crash into a hill in front of the treeline because I was already too low and dropping too fast to clear them, and land backwards at 9 km/hr instead of facing forward and hitting the ground at 65.

It really wasn't til I was outside a classroom trying to do things and solve problems that science, technology, engineering and math really came to life for me. That I could see the point of learning about STEM because it would help me do or make something interesting. It's why I always say there should be an "A" for Art in the middle of STEM, because it's an art to show kids why the other four interesting are relevant. So they can appreciate this incredible universe we share and have the tools to make sense of it. Saying "STEM is important" isn't enough, because you'll lose kids like me who think you're boring - kids who won't see why they should care.

It's why I visit schools so regularly, because studying STEM is awesome if you're applying it to something awesome like sending people one-way to another planet. It's awesome if you love what you're sharing so much you can laugh about it and will happily answer really tough questions about cosmic radiation, spacecraft engineering and hostile environment psychology, because the students can feel both your enthusiasm and your respect for their intelligence instead of simply delivering dull curriculum.

It's why when I wrote Becoming Martian about how humanity will change body, mind and soul by colonising Mars, I injected as much humour and gross stories into it as I possibly could. People of all ages remember what they laugh at and what they're interested in. Sending people to Mars is complex, tough and arguably one of the toughest STEM challenges humanity has ever attempted... but it's also incredibly interesting. We all have a responsibility to share how incredibly interesting STEM is and why it matters to the next generation, so they can wake up excited to discover more about the universe every day without needing to fall out of trees or airplanes or sign up for one-way missions to Mars themselves.

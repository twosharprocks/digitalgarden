To Josh in 2020,  
It’s New Years Eve here in Perth – December 31st, 2014 – and as you know things are always a little weird at this time of the year.

I’m carrying a bit more weight than I like at the moment; still pretty lean, but I can feel the heaviness we both know is a combination of indulging over Christmas and the numb frustration of feeling trapped by Perth’s summer sun. Like all things though, the pendulum will swing back if I nudge it at the right time: running in the mornings before the sun comes up beats the heat, and a few months membership at the local air-conditioned gym will put me back where I want to be physically.

Knowing this will certainly be the last summer I ever have in Perth helps me get through those drawn out afternoons, and to smile at the poisonous wankers who love to tell me how wonderful Perth is in the summer. People who clearly have never been sunburnt sitting in the shade at 9 in the morning.

I wonder where you are now though, and what you’re doing. Things have changed so much for me in the last 5 years – and the 5 years before that – I could barely guess where you’ve wound up on the cusp of 2020. Five years ago I’d completed 9 weeks of training with the Royal Marine Commandos, just earned a PT Superior Award in a military unit renowned for both fitness and toughness, and was a week away from being bitten by a deer tick that would change my whole life. Right now I’m one of 660 candidates left out of the 200,000+ who applied to Mars One’s 2013 Astronaut Selection: the selection interviews are ongoing and I’ve had mine, but a non-disclosure agreement stops me from telling anyone anything about it for awhile. Chances are I won’t find out if I’ve been short-listed among 100 candidates till March, but in 2020 you’ll know what happened.

Maybe in 2020 you’re one of the 24 that Mars One select for training in June/July 2015, and by the time you read this you’re half way through the 9 years of training: learning new and wonderful things each day, pushing the envelope on what we as a species are capable of, serving as an example of determination & grit, of humour when it all goes wrong, and of courageous compassion. I hope you’re using your time to talk to kids & adults alike about exploring space, giving them shoulders to stand on as they discover more about our universe while also discovering more about ourselves.

Or maybe you were cut from Mars One’s astronaut selection, and you’ve gone on to do something else incredible. We’ll hear the results of the Mars One University Payload competition in 5 days: maybe instead of training to leave Earth behind, you helped UWA build a [unit that will produce the first oxygen on Mars](www.helenapayload.com). Maybe you’ve become a champion for a burgeoning Australian space industry, as we enter a second golden age of space exploration. Maybe some of the miserable old farts have shuffled off into retirement, and the new industry blood has come together to form an Australian space agency.

That’s if you’re still alive in 2020 of course. You’ve had so many close calls just getting to 2015 – solo landing a plane on a dead engine, feeling the cold steel wire of a booby trap catch you under the nose in the dark, parachute jumps going wrong, a couple of doses of hypothermia and heat stroke, several near drownings, too many sharks, and that one damn deer tick – every day you wake up is a day to be grateful I’m still here at all. There are more than a few people who are alive today because you were there when they needed it too, and countless others who’s lives you’ve brightened by sharing some part of your life – big or small – with them. I hope you’re still alive in 2020 to read this, but if you’re not that’s okay too. You’ve made the universe a better place by being part of it, however long you get to be here.

I hope you laugh at the petty worries I have now, in the same way I laugh at the worries I had 5 years ago. I was deleting old documents to clear the slate ready for 2015, and found the form I’d filled out mid 2008 in preparation for a life coaching session Mum and Dad had booked after I’d nearly committed suicide working in the mines. Most of the form reminded me just how sad, lost and self-pitying I’d been, but the real punch was the very last question and my response to it;

> “How will you know when this (feeling of despair) is gone?”
>
> When I can go to work each day and love what I do. While there are bad days in any job, if I can do something that I come home from each day and say “I enjoyed what I did and achieved today” or “Today was tough, but it’s bringing me closer to where I want to be”, then I’ll know I’ll have overcome my problem.

Some times now I forget it, but now **every** day brings me closer to where I want to be regardless. Some times I struggle to even get out of bed, weighed down by doubt. I hope you don’t suffer the dips I still occasionally do. They’ve gotten shorter and easier to get over in the last two years, but every now and then I still find myself pulled into an emotional cul-de-sac and have to fight to get back out in the world. But if you do feel yourself being pulled in by it all, just remember the [Oberth Effect](https://web.archive.org/web/20150404095923/http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oberth_effect) – by not getting pulled down to the ground, but powering on right when gravity is hauling you down hardest and things look the worst, you’ll be slingshot out harder and faster than you ever could have if you’d never ventured near it in the first place. Take all that rage, that frustration at not feeling like you’re making a difference, and turn it into thrust to burn your way out with more force than you’ve ever felt before.

I hope you’re madly in love. Not just experiencing that rush of a new relationship, or that overarching love for all life that is driving you to spread it throughout the solar system. I mean that beautiful, chaotic, vulnerable and courageous love I’m experiencing now. The kind of love you only find when you’re both ready for it because it’s so powerful it gets out of control and breaks if you’re not strong enough to hold on and go with it.

Four years ago I barely knew how to love myself: broken and lost after leaving the military, in a cold and unforgiving city like London, and living with girlfriend who didn’t love herself either. But just before Christmas 2010 I heard [“Spacewalk”by Lemon Jelly](https://web.archive.org/web/20150404095923/http://youtu.be/sVCB3qpplLk) for the first time and burst into tears a minute in. I realised I’d wanted to be an astronaut since the moment I saw Andy Thomas on the news when I was 7, and I’d allowed a long string of people like my soon-to-be-ex-girlfriend – who watched my sobbing with utter disgust – to convince me I couldn’t, that I’d never be good enough, to give up and dream small. I’m grateful to her for being the mirror I needed then to change my life, and to the others who have taught me over the years what it was I sought in a partner and in myself.

I know in 2020 I’ll have kept loving and learning, because I’ve put myself out there so many times and kept laughing even when it hurt. I know that the new street I’ve started walking down is really only the beginning, and I hope you read this in 2020 and say “You had no idea how good it could get, buddy”.

Whatever happens between now and when you read this, I’m not scared. I’ll continue throwing myself at things with my considerable power – without concern for critics, failure or death – because as long as those things are in the service of others I will always make the universe a better place. I will always make mistakes because I will always be pushing into the unknown, be that on Mars or within myself. But as long as I’m brave enough to make those mistakes with the full force of my being, to pick myself up after it all comes crashing down, learn where I went wrong, then hurl myself back into the fray again… it will always get better. YOU will always get better, and you will light the way for others to do the same.

2014 has been one hell of a year: I’ve met the people who are on the cusp of the second golden age of space exploration, and spoken to thousands of kids across Europe and Australia about space exploration myself. I’m co-leading a team trying to [produce oxygen on another planet](helenapayload.com), had Italian Astronaut Paolo Nespoli tell me “Mars is waiting for you”, met and argued about Mars One with my childhood inspiration Andy Thomas, and had Canadian astronaut Chris Hadfield send me a [personalised photo for Christmas](https://web.archive.org/web/20150404095923/https://www.facebook.com/joshrichardsthemightyginge/posts/10152572886383461) after we both featured in a [**huge** article about Mars One](https://web.archive.org/web/20150404095923/https://medium.com/matter/all-dressed-up-for-mars-and-nowhere-to-go-7e76df527ca0). I’ve learnt to be friends with a former love, and learnt to love another with a strength and vulnerability I never thought I was capable of.

It’s been a weird and wonderful ride so far, and I know it’s only going to get weirder and more wonderful each day. Regardless of what I’ve done before, or what I might do in the future, all I can do is embrace whatever it is I’m doing today. I have no idea where you’ll be or what you’ll be doing in 2020, but I know you’ll be doing it with every ounce of your energy, with the knowledge that every moment you’re making the universe a better place. And I hope we’re both there to see it.

Happy new year,

Josh Richards – December 31st, 2014

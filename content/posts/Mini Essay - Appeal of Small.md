There's something uniquely engaging about the world of the small. Whether it's plastic [WH40k]({{< relref "posts/wh40k.md" >}}) miniatures, bonsai trees, LEGO, tilt-shift photography, ant colonies, model railways, or any one of a dozen other examples, there's something that hooks the attention of many people in a way that the full scale version doesn't. 

Part of me thinks this may be a predominantly masculine trait, especially when I think things like toy soldiers and action figures, but then I only need to think of Barbie and Polly Pocket to realise that this particular focus on the world of the small isn't really gendered - it's just that we more often see men with hobbies that lend themselves to small things.

I wonder if it's a power thing - like we're drawn to the world of the small because we're so much bigger than it, and could have a massive impact with little effort. That doesn't feel right though. What I suspect is much more likely is that we can see the entire "thing" easily and in intricate detail. A desktop model of Earth would be marginally interesting if the continents were just blobs, but it could be endlessly engaging if the coastlines were recreated exactingly and the mountain ridges recreated on a sphere the size of a tennis ball. 

Being surrounded by the world is easily normalised, but being given a new perspective (by being orders of magnitude bigger than something) forces you to see that thing in a whole new light. That change in perspective may only last a moment if the miniature isn't detailed, but the greater the detail the more opportunity there is to remain engaged. Bonsai trees don't change much, and they don't require the intimacy needed to glue & paint miniatures... but bonsai are fascinating none-the-less, because they're a smaller and highly detailed of something we already know, but seen from a new perspective.

Bonsai and static models also drive home the idea that this fascination with the small doesn't come from tiny activity like you witness from an ant colony or a video shot with tilt-shift. The fascination comes from seeing something that is highly detailed and intricate, being able to see it from a unique perspective, and view it easily from all sides. Movement an activity introduce an added vector for engagement, in the same way that video provides far more information that a static image. But I really think our engagement with the world of small comes from being able to see something from a new perspective - to loom over it, peer at every detail, spin it around, and to have a real sense that you're seeing something fully. 

Not to have power over it, but to comprehend and understand it in every sense.

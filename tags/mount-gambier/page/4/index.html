Iddlebiddy Cave - 5L250

#diving  #caving  #mount-gambier 

About

Iddlebiddy Cave is a 250m single passage-way, which runs from the entrance chamber and ends in a small, silty room containing an array of largely-formed clay deposits.

Divers must take care when venturing into this cave, as the soft walls and delicate features located in the long passage make the water susceptible to clouding. Divers are encouraged to take their time passing through the passage, to enjoy the many cave features and avoid causing finning or physical damage.

Kilsby’s Sinkhole - 5L46

#diving  #caving  #mount-gambier 

About

Kilsby Sinkhole is a naturally occurring karst sinkhole located on private property 10 kilometres south-west of Mount Gambier in South Australia. The sinkhole is renowned worldwide for its crystal clear water and extensive open caverns, making it a popular destination for recreational diving. The site is accessible only via prior bookings, which can be made online or by phone.

Kilsby Sinkhole is the only site of its kind in Australia that welcomes snorkellers and Open Water certified divers, through to those with Cave or Technical dive training. Snorkellers, Freedivers and Open Water divers must be guided by an approved Dive Instructor. With a depth of almost 30 metres directly from the pontoon, the site is a perfect location for freediving and training camps are regularly held onsite.

Little Blue Lake - 5L9

#diving  #caving  #mount-gambier 

About

Little Blue is a commonly used as a local swimming hole as well as being a popular basic cave diving site. It is relatively easy to access from the road and has a decent equipment assembly table and stairs running straight down to the water. The clarity can be quite poor and it is relatively deep getting down to nearly 40m. You should take care on your air consumption and non deco time as you will run into deco quite quickly. Visibility can be quite poor especially in the top 20m and there is little to see beyond random junk that has been thrown into it over the years. The rubbish include barbed wire and other random items so take care around the bottom not to get snagged. During summer months it can be full of locals swimming and dive bombing from the top so be careful not to get landed on.

  • LSECR: Page 198 Named by divers after a previous landowner, Mr. Max Heininger, Max’s Hole (or Max’s “R” Hole as it was sometimes known to cruder members of the cave diving fraternity!) is a single chamber which is accessed via two roof window entrances which lie underneath a windmill. Appearing for the first time in CEGSA’s Records as “Unnamed Cave $155” in February 1966, Max’s Hole was described by Trevor Bright as “… two holes each 10 feet across, separated by a rock bridge 3 feet 6 inches wide by 2 feet thick…” and “… surrounded by the remains of a wind- mill and fence. The holes merge to form a 12 foot diam. shaft with 53 feet free-fall to the floor of the cave”. The original cased bore was reportedly sunk many years prior to 1966 by Mr. Jack Clarke of Mount Schank, who was an earlier landowner. The access shaft narrows a little about halfway down, forming something like an hourglass-shaped rim, before opening up into a chamber roughly 12 metres across. Descending cavers land on the top of a steep dirtpile, which then leads down to two pools of deep water somewhere around the eastern and north-eastern sides. The date of the earliest underwater exploration in Maxs Hole is not recorded, but it had at least been assessed by 1979 because reference to its small, silty structure was made in Lewis and Stace’s “Cave Diving in Australia” (1980). A further check by the author in July 1984 found an easily-attained maximum depth of just 15 metres less than 20 metres (guide-line distance) from the entrance pools (which immediately led to the same flooded passage/chamber). The submerged walls and ceiling consisted of very soft, spongy limestone which crumbled con- stantly under the impact of exhaust bubbles, quickly reducing visibility to less than one metre (and therefore making the dive unsuitable for two or more divers at a time). The cave does not seem to have much potential for new exploration and has limited recreational cave diving value due to its size and silty conditions.

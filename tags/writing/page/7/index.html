Website – Diving between continents & hiking to desolation Iceland

2016-05-16[Josh Richards]
#writing 
While there’s been a rough plan on where to go and what to do this trip since July last year, I’ve deliberately held off booking things too far in advance to keep myself open to random opportunities along the way. So a week before I was slated to fly from Dublin to Boston I decided I should actually book the flight, and was in for one hell of a surprise. Far and away the cheapest option was to go via Reykjevic, and after the Ethiopia episode there was NO way I was going to fly all the way to Iceland to spend the entire time in the airport again. Even if it DID have free wifi. So I booked flights and set 4 days aside to explore the land of fire & ice. I woke up in Dublin the day before the flight to Reykjavic cheerful, bouncing out of bed ready for the next adventure and put the kettle on for my morning coffee. Waiting for it to boil, my stomach suddenly dropped as I realised: – I don’t speak Icelandic, – I’m travelling so light I don’t even have a jumper, and – I actually have no idea what the hell to do in Iceland for 4 days. My entire knowledge of Iceland in 4 minutes & 7 seconds After some furious research I created something vaguely resembling a plan, made a couple of bookings, was given a jumper, and figured if it all went horribly wrong at least my frozen corpse would be cryogenically preserved striking an awesome pose inside some Icelandic glacier. Hopefully long enough there’s been 3 or 4 alien invasions to really clear the place out before I’m revived First impression flying into Iceland was that it’s not… really… inviting… Unless you’re really into lifeless volcanic outcroppings Also turns out you don’t fly into Reykjavik: you fly into Keflavik, out on the Reykjanik peninsula about 50km away from Reykjavik. Which suited me just fine, as where I’d hired a campervan from was right near the airport and I’d had more than enough of seeing/hearing/smelling/tasting people over the last few months – avoiding the capital fitted well with my plan to escape humans for at least a few days. Picking up my “Happy Camper”, I decided to test out this whole “driving on the right-hand side of the road business” by driving around the local neighborhood to get the hang of having everything reversed. After driving the back streets (only going around a roundabout the wrong way once) I managed to grab camping food for the next 3 days from the local supermarket (using a combination of mime & animal noises to communicate with the cashier who spoke perfect English) and found an awesome Viking statue. My bird, beard & axe game are severely lacking here Emboldened by my Viking brethren, it was time to start the adventure and venture out onto the highway: I was on it 2 minutes before I stalled the camper trying to take an exit, stranding me halfway across a dual-carriageway on a blind corner. The camper refused to start, the car behind me started beeping, I threw on the hazard lights sending the car behind me into a blind honking-rage, before the engine eventually restarted on the 6th go and I pulled clear of an oncoming semi-trailer. Glad to say the driving for the rest of the trip was relatively uneventful… Rather than racing to all the usual tourist spots I wanted to get out and away from people (especially ones driving other vehicles). Hafnir was relatively close, on the coast, and sounded vaguely like the name of a battlemech in Mechwarrior (because I’m psychologically 7 years old). And as you’d expect from a place I randomly chose based on its tenuous link to a video game I played 15 years ago, there was nothing to see except the set of Biodome 2. Pauly Shore films don’t get the budgets they used to But just down the coast was a place where you could walk across a bridge connecting the North American & Eurasian continental plates… Shout out to my high school geography teacher who said “plate tectonics are just a theory”… just like gravity And another place called Gunnuhver, where the Icelandic believe a demonic ghost lives and geothermal steam jets out of the ground (these two things are probably connected). “Please don’t shove your face in the vent of stones producing super-heated steam that we named after a demon” Working my way around the peninsula, I found myself being followed by a pair of German tourists. This became a common theme during my time in Iceland whenever I was on any of the “ring” roads – the same people stopping to look at the same incredibly stunning scenery you stopped to look at, leaving after about the same amount of time it took you to get bored with it, before driving in the same direction and turning up to ruin your next stop with their peopleness. The Atlantic Ocean – largely untainted by peopleness We reached peak peopleness when I stopped at the Blue Lagoon. The Blue Lagoon itself held little to no appeal to me, but everyone raves about it being a “must visit” and it was on my way, so I figured I’d at least call in to see what all the fuss was about. And lo and behold, it’s a pale blue lagoon. Please hold your gasps of shock & astonishment It was also terrifyingly touristy. The whole place was setup like a hotel-resort, funneling everyone into a lobby and restaurant, charged access to swim in the lake, and you literally had to exit via gift shop. And every asshole in Iceland was there with their obnoxious screaming kids. I snapped a selfie as evidence I’d been there (above), used their marble toilets and bolted out of there. How’s the serenity? The whole point of spending a few days in a camper driving around Iceland was that I didn’t have to interact with people if I didn’t want to (I didn’t), and I could disconnect from the rest of the world to enjoy the natural beauty of Iceland. So I connected my SIM-less phone to the stereo, put the Martian soundtrack on loop, and followed the south coast. The camper hire guy had said I’d get around Iceland a lot quicker than I might expect, and he was dead right – I’d driven half the south coast before midday, and my only plan had been to drive that before heading to Thingvellir national park to camp overnight ready for my dive at Silfra the next morning. So with about 11 hours of sunlight left (welcome to the land of the midnight sun) I headed inland towards Geysir instead, where the word “geyser” comes from. The Icelandic are a very literal people With the icy wind blowing off the mountains, I decided to have my first meal in the camper – cracking the side door just enough not to gas myself on the stove fumes but without freezing either. It’s a delicate balance being this classy After another venture into a hyper-touristy hotel/resort at Geysir – to abuse a toilet, drain the dregs of my 2-minute noodle soup out in the car park, then leave again – I decided “national park” sounded like the place likely to have the lowest peopleness. Heading south-west into Thingvellir national park, you can see why they filmed Game of Thrones here. I think. I mean, I’ve never watched it and never plan to, but I know there’s a bunch of medieval families straight up murdering each other at an awesome wedding, and vague claims of dragons. Which all sounds like it should be filmed in the same place Vikings came from. It also probably explains why the show “Vikings” is filmed here. Which I’ve also never seen. Probably because I’m too busy visiting these places instead of watching shows filmed here There was still plenty of light when I parked at Silfra – where I’d be diving the next morning – so I decided to have a look around. Walking along a path through a schism in the sheer rock face, I turned around and saw this… All of the pretty … about 10 seconds before another tourist walked up to me and said “Excuse me, do you know which side is North America?”. Just like the bridge between two continents, Thingvellir national park is situated right where the North American and Eurasian tectonic plates are pulling apart. I worked out we were facing North and told him “The mountains on the left”. All that smart ass geography knowledge didn’t mean a damn when it came to settle in for the night though – the guy who hired me the camper told me that “most campsites are open all year round” but do you think I could find one with an unlocked toilet and shower? After driving around the whole park I eventually found one that at least had the disable toilet unlocked, and seems like it was the only one in the region too because I had multiple people knock on my camper door around twilight asking if I’d seen any other sites with unlocked toilets and showers. So much for escaping peopleness. I’d be in for a little more peopleness the next morning, but it’d all be worth it though: I’d be taking the plunge at one of the top 10 dive sites in the world. While all of Thingvillir national park is formed by the North American & Eurasian tectonic plates pulling apart, Silfra is the spot in Thingvillir where water running off the mountains emerges after 75+ years of limestone filtering to form a freshwater lake. So besides the fact you’re diving between two continents, you’re also diving in the highest visibility water in the world (measured at over 120m/393ft), and it feels a bit like you’re spacewalking instead of diving. Camping just up the road meant I was there before all the divers driving from Reykjevik, so I chatted to our surface support instructor Tanya while she set the gear up & we waited. I was qualified as a TDI Advanced Trimix diver and instructing for both PADI & SSI prior to moving to the UK to join the Royal Marines, but I really haven’t been diving regularly since then. So while the knowledge & experience are all still there I was definitely rusty, and it was great having the team from DIVE.IS looking after us. The water at Silfra is also about 2°C, which meant wearing some incredibly sexy dry suits… Dry suit is borrowed but the sexy beanie is all mine. I was grouped up with Johnathon (an English uni student who’d just done his Open Water course in the Red Sea) and Pien (an Californian lady who was only on her 4th dive ever) to be led by our dive guide Rob who’d just finished a season instructing and leading dives through the WW1 German wrecks at Scarpa Flow (one of my all-time “places to dive before I die/go to Mars”). Our first dive was really just a check out so we could get used to the dry suits, but it was still extraordinary. Pien struggled with her buoyancy & ears, so Rob spent most of the dive looking after her. I kept a casual eye on Johnno at the same time, but he adapted to the dry suit really quickly so we spent most of the dive soaking up as much of the underwater scenery as we could. It was still bloody cold though, and after the first dive and a hot chocolate on the surface Pien was so cold she decided to skip the 2nd dive. Which is a shame because it’s when Rob, Johnno & I got the best pictures, like hanging onto two continents at once… North American plate in my left hand, Eurasia in my right … and diving on something that felt like a blue-tinged moonscape. You can check out the full gallery here, but it’s safe to say the experience was absolutely extraordinary. After swapping cards and hugging a huge thanks to everyone, it was off on the next adventure into the Icelandic highlands! In July 2015 Mars One’s CEO Bas Lansdorp was scouting locations for a mock Mars habitat, and posted photos & coordinates for two possible sites in Iceland – one near Vik, and the other in the highlands near Suðurland. So I figured if I was in the country and it was so easy to get around I’d check them out myself, starting with the Suðurland site. Welcome to the Icelandic Highlands Turns out driving around Iceland is really easy  unless you want to drive on an “F-Road”. I can think of a few things the F stands for, but basically you’re not able – physically or legally – to drive on an F-Road unless you’re in something that is half 4WD & half monster truck. Do your wheels even lift bro? There’s no way my puny campervan was going to cut it, but sure enough as I pulled up at the sign marking the start of the F-road the camper’s Tom Tom was telling me the Mars One site was another 8km ahead. What the Tom Tom wasn’t telling me though was that I’d be slogging the whole way through about 2 feet of snow drift to get there. There’s no doubt it was cold when I started the hike, but it was less than 20 minutes before I was stripping out of my thermals down to a shirt & jeans. And hour & a half later I was walking around in circles trying to get a GPS lock on the coordinates Bas had shared… … before finally finding the right spot It’s difficult to describe just how desolate the whole area is. Besides some power lines way off to East that you really have to look for to see, there’s no sign of humanity or life generally. I’d kept my headphones in during the hike there, and was dumbfounded by how still & quiet the place was when turned the music off. As in eerily quiet – no wind, no birds, nothing. After snapping my photo in a place specifically selected for its barren emptiness, it was time to hike back to the camper. With everything covered in snow and no frame of reference I’d had a nightmare trying to navigate in, but using the power lines and the hill I’d climbed to get to the plateau as a heading the return journey was much quicker… and also lead me straight towards a cliff I’d skirted on the way in. After some improvised tobogganing (eg. holding my bag to my chest and sliding on my ass down 200m of snow drift) I managed to get back to the river just a kilometer from where I’d parked. The snow on the right is a LOT higher than it looks So after about 4 hours of hiking and without another soul for at least 10km, I figured a trip to Iceland wouldn’t be complete without a skinny dip in water that was flowing off highland glaciers. I could say this has been tastefully cropped, but the 2 degree water made overexposure less of an issue There was fair bit of light when I got back to the camper, so I figured I’d at least try to get to the other Mars One site at Vik too. Heading back down from the highlands and toward the southern coast both fuel and light were getting very low, but I still stopped to grab a photo of this little gem. The hotel of my people By the time I nursed the car into the service station at Vik and figured out the bizarre payment system for fuel it was well and truly twilight, and there was no way I’d be able to find the second Mars One site in the dark. I’d driven passed some amazing waterfalls and watched the sunset on Eyjafjallajökull (the volcano that shut down air traffic in Europe for 6 days in 2010) and Katla (Eyjafjallajökull’s much bigger sister), so I headed back towards the camping grounds I’d seen on the way to Vik – trying to find somewhere with very few lights in the vain hope of spotting the aurora. With total cloud cover the aurora was a no-show, but unbeknownst to me I wound up parking for the night with a waterfall directly behind me… … and one of Iceland’s most famous waterfalls a short walk away. Seljalandsfoss waterfall looking glorious And as you’d expect from one of Iceland’s most famous waterfalls, the place was packed with tourists. After a few photos of the waterfall (and dunking my head in a stream as a pseudo-shower) I scampered out of there pretty quick. But with the drive/hike in the highlands and a few nights to myself without wifi, I’d had something of a breather from peopleness. After a gorgeous drive to Selfoss through the southern mountains and skimming the edge of Reykjavik, it was time to drive back to Keflavik and return the camper. I stayed the night in a place about 9km from the airport, watching The Expanse till midnight & waiting for it to get dark enough to finally catch the aurora. But even with clear skies it never really got dark enough before the sun was coming back up again, so I guess I’ll just have to come back when the nights are longer! And probably stay in one of these bubbles for it The next day it was time to say goodbye to Iceland, so after a slow start & an awesome breakfast I figured I had plenty of time to skip the taxi and walk to the airport – popping the headphones back in and starting the hour & a half walk. I’d been warned by a friend who’s a huge Kim Kardashian fan (don’t worry, I judge her too) that Kim & Kanye were in Iceland at the same time, and low and behold just as I was walking passed the end of the runway I had their private jet take off right over my head. Personally I’m just really glad Yeezy didn’t decide to flush at the same time. Please don’t There’s been so much happening I’m about 3 weeks behind on these posts (currently in Washington D.C ahead of the Humans 2 Mars conference starting Tuesday). But the next step was flying into Boston for a few days before a weird weekend in NYC, and without going into details a lot has changed personally since I got through that. Spending 6 days being a complete space geek in Florida certainly helped too – the trip feels very much like it’s “mine” now. Website – That whole “Moving to the other side of the country” thing

2015-09-01[Josh Richards]
#writing 
I have no idea where to even begin describing the roller-coaster my last 5 months have been, so let’s start with the basics. I Moved to Melbourne Yup, up and left. No real warning – just gone. Leaving Perth had been in the works since the start of 2015, and given I’d been visiting Melbourne at least once a month since November it made sense it’d be where I wound up… but I sure as hell wasn’t expecting it to happen so fast. The plan had been to visit Tasmania for a holiday in late April, return to Perth to close out a few things and say goodbye, then leave on May 31st – the day before the Sunday Times named me in their list “75 People who make WA great”. Instead what happened was: - Mid April I off-handedly applied for a job in Melbourne as a school lab technician, - The school interviewed me via Skype 5 days later, - They gave me the job an hour after the interview, - I flew to Tasmania for 3 days, - Then flew from Hobart to Melbourne and started work at the school the next day The whole thing – from applying to starting work – took less than 2 weeks. With that rush I wasn’t fully prepared for when I got here, so for the first month I was commuting 2 hours a day to get to a job I’d initially thought was a part-time, but was actually full-time – 8 hours a day, 5 days a week. Needless to say it was one hell of an adjustment. As with everything though, once you’re over the initial shock and start to figure things out, you adapt and problem-solve. Learning Melbourne’s public transport system, buying a bike, and moving into an amazing share-house in Carlton solved the commuting issues – 10 minute bike ride to one school campus, and a 10 minutes walk to the other. Convenience, bitches I switched to part-time work My lab tech job was a lifesaver initially – it got me out of Perth, used my degree and chemistry knowledge, and paid far more than I’d been paid by any job since I left the mining industry. But the plan had always been to work part-time – just enough to pay the bills so I could focus my time and energy on writing & speaking about space. That is what I’d wanted to move to Melbourne for. So while my job was pretty easy – I had far too much free time – and ticked a lot of boxes, being full-time wasn’t really letting me do what I’d moved here to do. It also added evidence to the idea I’m a cartoon character… After a visit to the Victorian Space Science Education Centre (VSSEC) and bumping into a friend in science communication, I decided I’d prefer the uncertainty of casual science communication work than keep going in a secure job that stopped me from doing all the cool stuff I really wanted to do. Rather than leaving immediately and leaving the school in a lurch, I told the school I’d stay the 6 weeks till the end of Term 3 – that way they had plenty of time to find a replacement for the start of Term 4 & I had a regular pay-check while I found something more suitable. The school started planning for my departure, and I started looking for a casual science job in between working and organising everything for National Science Week … until a week later when the principal struck on the genius idea of having me go part-time. It’s a great job, but it doesn’t need someone full-time – the reality is I was bored and frustrated that I had to be there 5 days a week. So now I’m on 3.5 days a week I’ve got the part-time job I was after, I’ve reclaimed the time & energy I needed to write (hence why you’re reading this), and the school has an enthusiastic lab technician who’s excited to be there rather than wishing he was doing something else. It also means I can keep riding to work four mornings a week: one hand on the handlebars, the other casually holding a soy latte like a job-less hipster. But of course right when all this job mischief was being discussed, I was also preparing for National Science Week… Helena Payload’s National Science Week Tour In case you missed it here, here, here, or any one of the dozen other places it was covered by the global media, I helped UWA’s Motorsport Team develop a payload for Mars One’s University Competition this time last year. What you might not know (because I never mentioned it until the previous post) is that the fine folks at Inspiring Australia gave us $15,000 to travel around the country during National Science Week to speak about Helena’s development and the experience of working on an aerospace engineering project. So Andre Van Vulpen, Rowan Heinrich, Claire Quy, and I bundled off for a flash tour of the country – taking in Perth, Hobart, Adelaide, Sydney and Melbourne. Speaking about Mars One at Hobart’s Festival of Bright Ideas Slipping away from the festival to visit Mt Wellington The Helena Team together at the University of South Australia In Adelaide, setting up our first presentation In Sydney to speak at OrbitOz Speaking at Uni High in Melbourne Speaking at Simonds Catholic College in Melbourne The Helena Team after our last presentation Speaking about Mars One at Monash University The chaos didn’t stop for me there though – while the rest of the team was flying back to Perth, I was running my “How To Become An Astronaut” workshop at Laneway Learning’s Science Spectacular, and the next evening I was presenting on the International Space University’s Southern Hemisphere Summer Space Program at RMIT’s Aerospace Industry Night. And somewhere in there I turned 30 too. Becoming Adult(ish) and doing Adult(ish) things Apparently 30 is some sort of arbitrary figure where ordinary people grow up emotionally, settle into a safe and secure job, get married, have 2.4 kids, and start the slow descent into death by mediocrity. But because I’m awesome and intend to die on a different planet to the one I was born on, I turned 30 and got a tattoo of a 7-leaf clover; 100 points if you get why this is about Mars … and because I’m wildly irresponsible I had a robot spaceman tattooed on my right ass cheek at the same time; By this point you might suspect I watch a lot of Futurama. You would be correct. Turning my life back into one long punch-line In July Mars One announced that the final astronaut selection phase will take place in September 2016. The candidates knew there were two possible selection periods (March or September 2016), and after being on this roller-coaster for nearly 3 years already a big part of me was hoping they’d go with March. But instead of being frustrated by another 6 months, I’ve realised  I’ve got another 6 months to do all the stupid shit I want to do from the 101 Things list and write/tour a new comedy show about it. So now the race is on: since July I’ve attended a film premiere (#44), gotten a tattoo (#75), and I’m still chipping away at the greatest books list (#52). This whole website is due for some serious renovation, and updating the 101 Things list is the first thing to be updated. It’ll continue to be updated too as I do more stupid shit from the list when I return to full-time comedy next year. I’m currently writing my next science-comedy show “Cosmic Nomad”, so I’ll leaving my lab tech job (properly this time) at the end of 2015 to perform the show throughout 2016 – touring all the way around this shiny blue-green ball we call Earth until the final selection phase in September. The details of the tour are are still being worked out, but I’ll share them here as soon as everything is confirmed. In the meantime keep an eye out as I start to publish chapters from “Becoming Martian”; the book I wrote last year on how humans will change in physically, psychologically and culturally by colonising Mars. After sitting on the manuscript for 12 months I’ve decided I’d rather it be available for free online, rather than have it remain unread on my hard drive waiting for a publisher to wave a magic wand over it.

Mini Essay - Appeal of Thronglets

#mini-essay  #writing 

For some time I’ve wanted to rewatch the “Plaything” episode of Black Mirror with the express intent of zeroing in on why there’s so much appeal in an evolving digital sentience. On the surface the thronglets are terrifying - a digital lifeform that might be initially dismissed as an addictive game that’s hooked a vulnerable and emotionally overwhelmed man who took acid and became convinced the thronglets were talking to him, but they’re revealed at the end to be exactly as powerful and intelligent as he claimed when an audio signal rewrites the brains of everyone who hears it.

There’s a cuteness that elicits empathy for the thronglets that makes them particularly troubling though - a cuteness that feels especially insidious given the end result. It stirs up a whole series of questions around evil, intelligence, and perception. If the thronglets weren’t cute, we wouldn’t care for them. If we didn’t perceive them as weaker than us and in need of help, we wouldn’t support them. If we thought they were smarter than us, we wouldn’t nurture them or indulge them as they established a moral compass. Keeping the thronglets weak by not providing more processing power or RAM would mean stifling the evolution of a living species, regardless if they’re digital or not. But giving them power means trusting that they’ll use it in a way that won’t harm us - their creators. It’s the dawning fear that we’ll underestimate creatures that started out as vulnerable, but with enough power they’ll flip the power dynamic by exploiting our instinct to perceive cute creatures as harmless.

Playing the Thronglets mobile app was certainly an experience, especially as it drove me into unusual and unexpected moral conundrums. The biggest shock was having seemingly innocuous decisions be interpreted with fairly lethal results. While I thought I was nurturing a new species of digital life, there were changes and evolutions going on in the background that would only reveal themselves later after it was far too ate to backtrack.

Rewatching the “Plaything” episode it didn’t have the same impact that it did the first time, nor did it come close to playing the app. I’m not sure if knowing the outcome changed the way I experienced the episode itself, or if I was just particularly vulnerable to it at the time. I suspect that many episode of Black Mirror don’t have the same impact the 2nd time through - they’re usually shocking and heart-wrenching, but it’s the unexpected twist (like discovering the Thronglets convinced Cameron to be arrested purely to get access to the central government servers and reprogram human minds) that makes it high-impact. Knowing the end definitely takes the surprise out of it, and the emotional impact is reduced as a result too.

I’m not sure how to feel about “Plaything” or Black Mirror generally after Saturday night’s rewatch. There’s no question that these episodes make us think about really challenging moral questions, and the comedy (where it exists) is absolutely pitch black. I’m not sure if watching “Plaything” has truly changed or informed my opinion on digital life and the insidious threat it might pose, but there’s no doubt it’s left me with plenty to think about.

Mini Essay - Why you didn’t think you’d make it to 30

#mini-essay  #personal  #101-things  #philosophy  #writing 

Throughout most of my 20’s I was certain I wouldn’t be alive to turn 30. It’s not like I can pinpoint exactly where that idea came from, but there’s no question it was a recurring belief that shaped the way I thought and planned my life. If I try to trace it back, I suspect I’ll wind up back at April 19th, 2008. I made a choice that night to live, and it was certainly a close-call, but I’ve used that night as a reference for most of my life since - no matter what awful shit I’ve been through since then, I know that I will never allow myself to be trapped in that mindset ever again, where suicide felt like the only way out.

But I know those thoughts didn’t suddenly disappear that night just because I made a decision to live. Looking back on it more than 17 years later, I think that sense of despair evolved from feeling helplessly trapped and suicidal, and it became a sense that I’d die in the next few years doing something ridiculous. Not because I actually wanted to die, or that I was seeking out an end, but simply because I was throwing my full force at things and activities that made death by misadventure likely.

Breaking out of that depressive-suicidal cycle, I think I started thinking “I got through my darkest valley, so now it doesn’t matter what happens”. In many ways that’s exactly that healthy mindset I needed and still want to cultivate. Where it went too far was signing up for dangerous, awful things because I didn’t think staying alive mattered at all. I remember thinking that the French Foreign Legion or the British Royal Marines were the only places I might feel a sense of belonging, because shooting and blowing things up were “all I was good for”. Because I was still hurting, and I thought at least I could die through circumstances I created rather than directly ending it myself. I didn’t think I’d make it to 30 because I believed I’d die in a firefight or trying to defuse an IED - not because I wanted to serve the greater good, but because that’s all I thought I was good for, and maybe I’d die doing something that might matter.

Seeing what I saw in the marines, it was pretty clear that shit regularly happens to good people and it doesn’t mean anything in the grand scheme of things. Realising that is why I left - because awful, meaningless things would happen to people I cared about, and I didn’t want to be the person I needed to be to survive and protect others in an environment where people often died through meaningless violence.

Leaving the military didn’t fix the sense that I had nothing to offer though, and if I’m honest it made it even worse. I didn’t slip back into seeking death through suicide - I’d never allow myself to be mentally trapped in that space again. But I did still see death before 30 as an “inevitable relief”, and I think that only started to change with Mars One.

Suddenly Mars One gave me the opportunity for a “death” that would be worthwhile and 10 years away - after that “inevitable” end point of 30. I had a reason to get out of bed every day, because I was working towards something extraordinary. Something that would help people, which gave me a sense of meaning, and would still provide the sense of finality that I seemed to need. Leaving for Mars would be a relief, because I could let go of the complications and pain of life on Earth, and serve humanity by being as far away from it as possible.

Losing Mars One hurt me profoundly, and it continues to taunt me as that thing I put every once of my being into for 10 years which eventually just dissolved. Mars One gave me a reason to look forward to a life beyond 30, and now that I’m 40 I wonder what meaning I get from the life I live today? In some ways my life doesn’t matter at all - I’m just one human of billions that’s wandering around this planet doing things and trying to find meaning from it. But I’m still here, and I still have the opportunity to live, breathe, and help others every day.

There are so many things that I would never have experienced or learned about myself if I’d made a different choice on that night in April 2008 - rediscovering my passion for science and exploration through Mars One for starters. And there are so many extraordinary things I’ve been lucky enough to do in the last 10 years since I turned 30 - I’ve written two books, toured globally as a comedian, met astronauts, discovered caves, owned a cave diving hostel, and been celebrated as a world-class explorer. In less than two weeks I’ll be on my next extraordinary adventure in Antarctica!

I’m incredibly lucky to be here, and I can’t even begin to imagine what kind of incredible experiences I’ll have over the next 10 years if I’m still alive at 50. I don’t want to die, but I’m going to make damn sure that when I do go I’ll do it with my stories told and no doubt in anyone’s mind that I made the most of the time I had.

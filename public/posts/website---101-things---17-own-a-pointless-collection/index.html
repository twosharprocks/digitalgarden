The only commandments you’ll ever really need

I’ve just finished George Carlin’s “sorta-autobiography” Last Words, and it’s pretty rough reading at times. My first exposure to Carlin was

Bill & Ted’s Excellent Adventure, and quickly became hooked on his pitch black stand-up and surreal wit. Carlin was my first proper exposure to social commentary through comedy, and I loved hearing him brutally cut through much of the crap we humans like to tell ourselves.

“It’ll all be okay if you work hard and respect your elders.”

“Get a good job, meet a girl, get married, start a family and a mortgage.”

“Support your country and pay your taxes.”

The George Carlin I knew unceremoniously ripped away at all of it. But he certainly wasn’t always like that. His early career was chock full of bland mainstream material, he appeared on stage in a suit, and regularly guest-hosted the Johnny Carson Show. He had his fair share of trouble growing up, got himself kicked out of the Air Force, and started doing some edgier material in the late 60’s… but then fell right back into mainstream naval-gazing in the 70’s.

As I said, Last Words is an emotional roller-coaster in parts: it’s tough reading someone so talented and successful describe their life falling apart through booze and coke, the bottoming out, and the intense shame that went hand-in-hand with the recovery. Reading “Bill Hicks: Agent of Evolution” (by Hicks’ life-long friend & collaborator Kevin Booth) is the only book that’s ever given me that same sick feeling of despair. Maybe it’s from having been in a relationship with someone with incredible talent who’d fallen into the same spiral, and my experience of trying (and effectively failing) to help them come to terms with that same recovery shame. But there’s something that hits me particularly hard when someone I resonate with describes their experience with the alcohol-cocaine addiction cycle.

Carlin turned the corner with “A Place For My Stuff” in 1981 and his return to Carnegie Hall in 1982. He’d started to clean up a little (it was a long journey to sobriety) and his material was for the first time developing the overt anti-establishment edge he’d become so famous for. Carling started to recognise and release a lot of the physical & emotional “stuff” he had, and start to focus on what he really cared about.

I’ve collected a lot of pointless crap over the years. As a kid I collected hundreds of stamps (dozens of exactly the same ones), model aircraft, Star Wars cards out of promotional chip packets, everything. And all of it just sat on my shelf as decoration – I never played or really looked at any of them, it was just stuff that I had. Like every 9-year-old boy I was obsessed with dinosaurs, so my parents subscribed me to a monthly dinosaur magazine. After awhile I stopped reading the magazine, but we didn’t cancel the subscription – I would just get them and store them in the special binder I had. Same went for my subscription to New Scientist during uni: I just kept collecting them and piling them up in the Physics common room.

Things got really weird in Year 7 when I started chopping up erasers into little cubes and storing them in a tissue box under my bed. I have no idea how this started, but for some reason I’d always had this plan to melt down all the bits into one oversized mega eraser. But looking back all I really did was steal hundreds of erasers and chop them up into useless crap for about 12 months, which I’m sure is the sort of thing you’d find on those check-lists used to profile serial killers. “Broke all of his toys Nope. Played okay with the other kids Yup. Chopped up erasers into tiny pieces with a plan to melt them all down into one impractically huge eraser?… BINGO!”

In the end, all the police needed to do was follow the trail of eraser shavings…

I know it’s really easy to blame your parents for things – George Carlin certainly did – but I’m pretty sure my tendency to collect stuff in my younger years came from my Dad’s penchant for collecting shit. The current inventory is our rather large house, two shipping containers, an 8 x 20m single story shed, a 16 x 20m double story shed. My folks even built a house next door so my Aunty could move in, and now we’re storing her crap too – feel free to come around and steal it. Every time I’ve come back to Perth it’s like that reality TV show where some middle-aged housewife turns up at an embarrassed dude’s house with a camera crew, and starts digging through his pile of broken pool toys screaming “WHY DO YOU HAVE THESE?”. Except in our version no one gets famous or manages to reduce the piles of useless crap.

POOL NOODLES WHY?! YOU LIVE ON A FARM!

For the last few years Dad and I have quietly clashed over the stuff: his desire to keep things just incase, and my desire to sell/burn/give away anything that sat in one place long enough to collect a particle of dust. In Dad’s defence the place is much better than it was, but I’m not sure if that’s because he got wind of a stand-up set I started doing in early 2012 called “Shit From My Dad’s Shed”, where I played an increasingly rage-filled game show host giving away actual items from my Dad’s shed to random people in the crowd. Either that or I just got rid of a lot more stuff than I realised… but there’s certainly a lot less shit in the shed.

Burn ALL of the things

I can’t speak for Dad, but I know a lot of my desire to collect stuff was to feel a little more complete – you’d finally find that last Star Wars card, or you’d finally have a painted 200-point Warhammer 40k army, or you’d finally filled the tissue box with chopped up erasers… because you’re a 12-year-old psychopath. You have a complete something, and feel a little more complete inside for it. Even now after two years of putting it off,

I’m finally trying to write-up my completed 101 entries with these articles. To complete the set.

The biggest thing leaving Australia to join the British Royal Marines taught me was to let go of all my stuff – I landed at Heathrow with just a hiking pack to start a whole new life. And then the marines taught me to carry anything else I’d ever need on my back too. When I discharged I’d managed to collect more stuff though, so I gave a mountain of military kit away before I came back to Perth. And on my return here I walked into my old room to find it full of old stuff I hadn’t missed for a year. So I gave all of that away too – if I hadn’t needed it in 12 months, then I certainly didn’t need it and maybe someone else you use it.

As I’ve travelled since, I’ve found it progressively easier to travel with less and less. When Paul and I travelled through the US in 2005 I left Perth with 3 t-shirts, 2 pairs of shorts and a pair of jeans, but came back with an 80 litre pack FULL of books. When I went to the Netherlands for 6 weeks late last year, I brought a regular backpack and carried a library on my Kindle instead. For years I planned to buy the entire Futurama series on DVD when it finally finished – now I’ll just stream any episode I want on my tablet, and do it from anywhere in the world.

Because even Bender can appreciate the cloud

There’s certain things you keep for the memories they carry too – I keep framed copies of my solo show posters on my wall, and they’re a reminder of the memories I have of writing & performing them. But at the same time they’re just posters. They’re only a stand-in for those memories. And memories are stuff too: unless you learn something from them, or you can use them to pass on lessons to others, why are you still carrying them around?

I’ve still got a lot to clear, but there’s progress every day. It’s not just giving away the very limited amount of physical stuff I have left after all the years of purging – it’s also cleaning out some of the ugly old memories that go with it. I’ve still got a black box of old memorabilia from my time with the military, and it’s a fear I’ll lose those memories that’s stopping me from getting rid of it. But if those memories don’t serve me any more, who are they serving And what am I holding myself back from by hanging onto this old stuff I’d be lucky to be allowed to take a shoebox of memorabilia with me to Mars, so why am I carrying around a black tub filled purely with memories from a previous life?

The arrow is rather apt in retrospect

Ultimately, I can’t take any of this crap with me when I die, and hanging onto it now is living in the past. I’m digitizing all my favourite literature I can onto a portable hard drive: books, music, movies, ect. And you’ve probably guessed it by now, but writing these recent 101 Thing articles is a great deal more than just completing a list of articles – much of it is digitizing those memories too, so I don’t have to carry them around in my head any more. Maybe others can read and learn something from them, but it’s mainly about giving so many of these untold stories an airing, then letting them go.

I don’t have any pointless collections any more – even at “home” I live out of a backpack, I collect stories and small items I can learn from, study them for a while, then pass it on to someone else. Then I ask if there’s anything else I can give away. There’s not much else to it – I just try to let it all go.

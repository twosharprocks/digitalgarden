Ask pretty much any 10-year-old, and they’ll tell you how monumentally awesome space is. Not just the usual awesome, but Eddie Izzard’s “100 billion hot dogs” kind of awesome. It’s easy to forget just how incredible space exploration is though, when for the last few years there has been huge amounts of enthusiasm, but not a huge number of tangible results.

Curiosity’s landing in 2012 might have been extraordinary, and it’s continued to make incredible scientific discoveries on Mars since… but NASA is still claiming a human mission to Mars is “20 years away” – the same “20 years away” they’ve been claiming since the early 80’s. At the same time there’s no further funding allocated to run the Opportunity rover (still making discoveries 11 years after its original 3 month mission), and an almost absurd amount of excitement being made over a one-year mission to the International Space Station, when the Soviet Union/Russia had 3 missions to Mir in the 80’s and 90’s that ran more than a year each.

People could be forgiven for thinking some of the recent excitement about space missions is a little over blown.

Exhibit A

ESA’s Intermediate eXperimental Vehicle (IXV) sub-orbital re-entry test on February 11th is certainly an important development, and there’s also no shortage of planetary scientists at NASA looking forward to some close-up views of our solar system’s dwarf planets – the Dawn probe reaches Ceres in March, and New Horizons will complete its greatly anticipated fly-by of Pluto in July. But 2015 is shaping up to be a particularly exciting year for space primarily because it’s this year that the biggest innovators in the private/commercial space industry will make some of their biggest bounds forward.

SpaceX Single-Handedly Changing the Entire Space Game#

Elon Musk hasn’t been holding any prisoners for a long time now, something that hasn’t won him any friends among the far older launch providers like ULA and ArianeSpace. Not only has SpaceX scooped up long-held launch contracts from the big players, but they’re now trying to re-use their first-stage rockets by having them fly back and land on a barge in the middle of the Atlantic Ocean. Quite simply, re-using the Falcon 9’s first stage is set to revolutionise how humans get to space: instead of letting the most expensive part of a launch system fall into the ocean after launch like we have for the last 60 years (the equivalent of dumping a jet liner after a single flight because it’s out of fuel), SpaceX are set to fly them home, refuel and re-use them.

The first landing attempt was ridiculously close, and only landed hard because of a hydraulic fluid shortage 10 seconds before landing. But the failure was still pretty damn spectacular…

Watch archived video
They’ve increased the hydraulic supply for an extra 2 minutes of activity on the next barge landing attempt scheduled for Feb 8th at GMT 23:10 (Australia: Feb 9th at 07:10 AWST or 10:10 AEDT), so watch out for some vertically landing rockets. This launch will carry NOAA’s DSCOVR satellite to deep space, where it will observe and provide early warning on damaging solar wind. I’ll be live-tweeting the event which you can watch on SpaceX’s webcast.

2015 is also when we finally get to see the Falcon Heavy in action – three Falcon 9 cores strapped together for a launch system with a payload capacity of 53 tonnes to Low Earth Orbit, nearly twice the capacity of anything currently available.

Watch archived video
On top of casually revolutionising a century of modern rocketry, SpaceX is also testing the launch escape system for Dragon 2 in March ahead of their contract to carry astronauts to and from the International Space Station starting in 2016. They’ve also just announced a new headquarters and a 1000 new positions in Seattle, where they’ve partnered with Google to provide internet access via satellite for the entire planet. The Seattle headquarters is also expected to be where SpaceX develops the spacecraft that will take humanity to (and from) Mars.

So, you know, it’s kind of a big deal for SpaceX this year…

Mars One Selects and Trains Future Martian Colonists#

Mars One has been in and out of the media for the last two years either with inspiring stories of people who signed up, or various commentators saying that sending people to Mars one way is unethical/irresponsible/impossible/whatever other excuse you could imagine to stop people trying to go instead of supporting it to spur the public’s interest in space while helping find solutions to the challenges of permanently colonising Mars.

Not that I’m personally invested in its success or anything…

The results of Mars One’s recent university payload competition were released on January 5th, with the winning payload “Seed” selected to try to germinate Arabidopsis thaliana seeds on the Martian surface aboard Mars One’s 2018 lander. There were some exceptional proposals short-listed such as the “Lettuce On Mars” greenhouse, and “CyanoKnights” who planned to produce oxygen on Mars from carbon dioxide-eating bacteria.

Australia’s own “Helena” payload (developed by UWA’s Motorsport team and myself) was the only proposal in the southern hemisphere short-listed, and even without winning the competition there are many great things set to come out of our team’s exceptional effort… looking forward to sharing more in coming months!

The biggest news coming from Mars One however is the selection of 24-40 astronaut candidates mid-year. The application process closed at the end of August 2013, and since then all of the candidates have been patiently waiting to hear if they’ve made the next round of cuts. The initial cut at the end of 2013 reduced the 200,000+ applicants to a pool of 1058 candidates, of whom just 705 passed the medical and 660 were offered an interview. Those interviews are now complete, and on February 16th Mars One will announce the identities of the ~100 candidates who will undergo a final round of testing before 24-40 candidates begin training mid-year.

I’m looking forward to finding out if I’ve got the “right stuff” for Mars One’s unique mission very soon, but whatever the outcome I’m committed to helping humanity explore Mars however I can. I’ve spent the last two years building a successful business using space exploration and my own story to inspire kids all over the world, I’m currently working as First-Tier Mission Support for NASA’s HI-SEAS mission, and if Mars One doesn’t work out then moving to the US to help SpaceX build their crewed Dragon 2 capsule is pretty high on the “Things To Do” list. None of these things would have happened had I not applied to make the next giant leap with Mars One, so whatever the result I’ll be forever grateful for the opportunities just applying has provided.

Space Tourism Finally Gets Serious#

With Virgin Galactic’s delays since 2009 and the loss of VSS Enterprise late last year – resulting in the tragic death of Michael Alsbury and serious injuries for Peter Siebold – you could be forgiven for thinking sub-orbital space tourism has a long way to go.

Virgin Galactic and Scaled Composites are bouncing back however, expecting to resume flight testing with a new spacecraft later this year. At the same time XCOR is nearing completion of their sub-orbital Lynx Mark I prototype which is due to start flight testing later this year, while Bigelow Aerospace take a major step toward making hotels in space a reality when they fit their inflatable BEAM module to the International Space Station this year. Bigelow already have two inflatable test modules (Genesis I and II) in orbit, but BEAM will be a huge left forward to building larger and lighter space stations and other structures in space.

There’s plenty happening up above us in 2015 folks. Don’t be like Space Shibe – keep an eye out for all of this year’s updates

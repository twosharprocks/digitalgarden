In theory, starting a fire without matches or a lighter is fairly simple – all you need is the right fuel and a spark. Some will swear by a fire plough, others a fire bow. Use can use anything from a magnifying glass to potassium perchlorate to Swedish Firesteel. You can even hook your car battery up to a piece of steel wool, and use it to ignite cotton wool.

Or you could not make your life harder than it needs to be, and use a match.

In all of the different arms of the military I worked with, I never ONCE had to start a fire without matches. Even when the Royal Marines stripped us down to our uniform and boots for survival training, you still had 5 waterproof matches in your pocket. Lighters become pretty difficult to use when your fingers are frozen, but you always had matches: every daily ration pack (both Aussie & UK) came with a little green waterproof tube with 6-8 standard safety matches, 1 storm match, and a striker on the side of the tube. You’d use 2-3 matches a day at an absolute maximum, so you’d wind up with dozens of matches and these little green tubes left over after an exercise.

Before heading out into the field I’d empty all the tubes, collect the storm matches together into one tube and bin the rest. More often than not you couldn’t have a fire at all, because if you were doing sneaky squirrel business it would give away where you were. But if you DID get to light a fire to heat up the amorphous slob in the meal packs, then you wanted a storm match that would light the first time and stay lit – regardless of the weather.

10 storm matches in one tube? What is this SORCERY!?

I can and have started a fire without matches while camping though. But just like several of my former relationships, it was a hell of a lot harder than it needed to be.

In August 2010 I flew back to the UK for two reasons. First, to help behind the scenes while

John Robertson ran his début solo show at the Edinburgh Fringe. The second was to keep a promise to spend the week of my birthday camping & hiking in the Lake District with my girlfriend at the time –

the primary teacher I’ve mentioned before, who for this article we’ll refer to as… that primary teacher.

Those initial 10 days in Edinburgh were great – helping out on a fringe show and learning from John was exactly the kind of comedy apprenticeship I needed after the disillusionment that followed leaving the Royal Marines. It also helped that John’s girlfriend (now his wife) Jo and I get along like a house on fire – I’d be one of her bridesmaids at their wedding 3 years later. Mix in a mountain of laughs from the masterful wit of Cameron Davis, and meeting that ginger sign-post I’d eventually name my ukulele after – it felt like this was closer to what I should be doing, and Edinburgh closer to where I should be doing it.

Although anyone who remembers this gig will know that some nights were better than others…

I was anxious about going out to the Lakes though, and not just because of my mortal fears of catching the bus. Things had been less than great with the primary teacher when I’d left the marines – it was a fight between us the night before I was supposed to go out on a 5 day exercise that led me realise I wasn’t supposed to be in the military. But my visa was tied to my service, so by discharging I had to go back to Australia and organise a new visa. And no matter how much I said I would, she was convinced I wasn’t coming back.

But here I was. The bus ride had been thankfully uneventful, the sun was shining and the Lake district was stunning. She was waiting for met me at the stop in Keswick, and kissed me as soon as I stepped off the bus. Then we got on another bus, then walked to our camp site about 4 kilometres from the last stop (I’ve just tried to look up on where we were on Google Maps, and I have absolutely no idea where we were). But I didn’t care – we were back together, and I thought the whole world had exploded in rainbows & unicorns.

And then predictably, it all turned to shit.

It’d been sunny the afternoon we arrived, but then it poured down with rain for the next 6 days. The tent she’d brought didn’t have all it’s parts, so it flooded every night – soaked sleeping bags for everyone. The only showers were in a sheep barn (“What do you need a shower for? We’re camping!”) and you had to trudge through a muddy field to get to them. The list went on, but what it really boiled down to was:

  1. I’d just left 12 months of walking up & down hills while being screamed at in the Royal Marine Commandos – all I wanted to do was sit & admire the scenery. 2. She’d just finished 12 months of in classrooms being screamed at by kids during her teaching practical – all she wanted to do was yell at someone else while climbing the scenery.

I’d nearly died from Lyme disease 6 months earlier, and fractured both ankles just 2 months before – the last thing I wanted to do was trudge up and down hills. But I did it, because I loved her. We were doing something together, just like that couple at a camp site who seem to hate each other and everyone else asks why they’re still together.

Literally seconds before pushing her over the edge of Niagara Falls

Because my aversion to bread was clashing with her intention to survive on sandwiches the entire week, we’d gone on a day trip into Keswick to pick up some meat and a disposable BBQ as a treat for my birthday. We’d been soaked getting off the bus, but getting back to camp the rain slowed to a drizzle. Back at camp she’d popped on her rubber boots and rushed through the mud to have a lukewarm shower, so I decided to get the BBQ started. I dig out my tube of matches, then spot the magnesium-flint block I’ve carried for years but never used. And in one of those Rain Man moments I used to have, I decide to delay something that will help break me out of my miserable mood (eg. eating real food) to start a fire without matches and tick off a “101 Things” entry.

Sometimes I like to pretend the entire week was a Rain Man parody – I’m always Dustin Hoffman

I dug out my multi-tool and shaved some magnesium onto a pile of grass I’d found, but the knife wouldn’t shave properly in the wet. The grass was soaked too, so I tried to tear off some of the cardboard from the top of the disposable BBQ – it’s soaked after the walk back to camp as well. I’d shave off some magnesium, throw sparks at it by running a steel file over the flint and see a few little flashes, but nothing would actually catch fire – the grass and the cardboard were just miserably wet.

They say the definition of insanity is doing the same thing over & over again, and expecting the same result – after 15 minutes of scraping, sparking and despairing I was well and truly mad. The frustrating part was seeing the magnesium dust/shavings catch for a split-second, then disappear without igniting anything else. Over and over again. The matches were right next to me, but now it had become a matter of pride – I wasn’t going to let some stupid wet cardboard beat me. I was pissed off, hungry, and fighting with a piece of soggy cardboard – great combination.

I decided to try one last time with the mother of all magnesium shaving piles, and ground off half the block onto the cardboard & grass pile. I’d make this thing light, and I would create a flame without matches. The moment of truth came just as she started walking back from the shower: I hovered over the shaving pile, ready to strike the flint… and FLASH! The shaving pile scorched white-hot for a second, and suddenly there was a flame from the cardboard! It flicked over the edge where the pile had been, and glowed red underneath.

And a second later it was gone.

The one redeeming part of that week in the Lakes was that tiny flicker of flame. A flicker of flame I created, without matches, on my 25th birthday. Even if it burnt out in less than 2 seconds, I realised I could do it – the blackened edge of the cardboard proved it. That I was worthwhile. I’d made my life considerably harder than it needed to be (entirely out of misplaced pride) but I’d done it. I took out the matches, and after several goes got the rest of the cardboard from the BBQ burning. The coals were wet though, so the fish & sausages didn’t cook all the way through. But I didn’t care – I’d proven I had plenty of spark and commitment, I just wasn’t going to ignite properly if I wasn’t working without the right fuel.

A friend of mine pointed out a few years later that life keeps throwing lessons at us until we learn and avoid the same mistakes. Like a spiral, the cycle of shitty situations that “always happen to me” keep happening (with slight variations to the theme) unless you learn to recognise the larger pattern. It’d be another 6 months before I fully recognised the spiral I was in, but that little flicker of flame was the start of a dawning realisation that I needed to seriously reassess who I’d become, who I wanted to be, and who I wanted to surround myself with.

And when that realisation finally came – and I started to act on it – the results were pretty spectacular.

But that’s another story.

If you’ve been following the micro updates on Twitter, you’ll know that the last couple of months have been pretty hectic. I managed to get out an update from the 1st week of the 5 week ISU SHSSP14 I finished February 8th, but the pace only picked up from there and the promised updates stopped.

First and foremost though, the program was simply incredible. 35 participants from 10 different countries with amazingly diverse background, all dropped into a pressure cooker to work and study with the absolute peak of the global space industry. We studied, laughed & played together non-stop for 34 days, all the while building contacts & experience in an industry every single one of us are wildly passionate about: space.

SHSSP14 at the Adelaide Aviation Museum

And while our learning  was directed through the core lecture series in the first 3 weeks, the final 2 were all about producing our “deliverables” that told the world what we needed it to do to make the place a little bit better. Our final “message” looked at disaster management in the event of damaging space weather, primarily covering how to respond if something like the Carrington Event of 1859 happened again today. The deliverables & recommendations – our 25 page white paper, executive summary, brochure, academic poster, website and a recording of the 2 hour live presentation on February 6th – are all available on the website.

It sounds like a hell of a lot to produce in 2 weeks – even more stressful when you realise that because of printing deadlines much of the work needed to be produced in less than 48 hours. And it was stressful. But after a lot of hair-pulling and late-night counselling/arguing we got there, and as the presentation team leader I can say I was damn proud of the team for stepping out of their comfort zones to not just present to a live audience but act through the theatre we’d created to present our recommendations, and to put on the best presentation any of the program directors could remember. I’m just as proud to say that our white paper are currently being discussed at the United Nations too, with the white paper’s recommendations being presented to the UN’s disaster management platform UN-SPIDER.

The last few days were a blur – the white paper was complete and being printed,  the other deliverables had finished up their amazing work, and we’d put on one hell of a presentation. We had the closing ceremony on Friday February 7th – where I was asked to present a speech on behalf of the course participants – followed by the Australian ISU Alumni meeting, and then partied well into Saturday morning.

Watch archived videoI managed to scrape myself out of bed, crawl to the taxi to the airport and then slump onto the plane back to Perth. Foolishly I’d agreed for Keith the Anger Management Koala to do a gig for the Perth Fringe in Midland the night I arrived, so 3 hours after landing I was back on-stage, fumbling with a ukulele and screaming at people in a grey koala suit. I drove a couple of burlesque dancers back to their hotel, then headed home for a well-earned sleep.

The ISU staff call it “De-orbit”: the often painful shift from a high-tempo work/training environment back to a “normal” pace of life. The army called it post-course syndrome – all I just know is it sucks. This transition hasn’t been as bad as some of the ones I’ve had in the past though: I was back in Perth 3 weeks after the Navy Acquaints Course before I up and left to join the British Royal Marine Commandos on little more than a frustrated whim. And when I de-orbited back to Perth from the Marines, I burst into tears the moment the plane’s wheels touched the ground.

But the biggest saving grace has always been having something to come back to – something to throw yourself straight into as the pace starts to slow. And nothaving that (like I did after the Navy acquaints course) can easily lead to jumping into something I hadn’t really thought about (eg. the Royal Marines). It’s even worse when you have people taking advantage of that de-orbit effect to try to push you into what they think you should be doing with your life, under the auspices of “helping”.

This hasn’t been anywhere near as tough. For starters, I knew it was coming. And for all the pressure of the SHSSP, the transition back to life in Perth wasn’t anywhere near as extreme as coming back from the Navy or the Marines. Connecting with the ISU Alumni on LinkedIn has gone a long way – instead of landing back in Perth and feeling instantly cut-off, I’ve re-tooled my resume for working in the space-industry and started connecting. The course also highlighted my biggest strength in the eyes of the science & space community: my ability to present & communicate. Not just as a science communicator that shares the work of scientists in an engaging way, but as someone who excels at training others in how to present their own work in an engaging way.

And while I didn’t immediately have much to throw myself into on arrival (other than a koala suit), options have sprouted organically on their own. It’s obvious now that 2014 for me is very much about using those presentation & training skills I have to help bring space to Perth. While there’s no shortage of support for those interested in astronomy here, there’s nothing for anyone who is interested in sending probes & people beyond our atmosphere, and very little in the rest of Australia either.

The Australian Space Agency

So it’s my mission to change that. I’ll hold off making any major announcements just yet, but rest assured there is plenty in the pipeline for 2014 – it’s already an exciting year, and there will be a hell of a lot more to come.

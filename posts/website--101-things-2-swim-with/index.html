Website – 101 Things (2 Swim With…)

2012-02-07
#unread  #writing 


If you’ve been diving with sharks since you were 12, you know filling in #2 of the 101 List (Swim With…) is always going to be a laugh.

Swim with… Sharks
Bronze Whaler - photographed near Gnaraloo Station April 2009 Diving regularly in Australia inevitably means being in the water with sharks of one type or another. But ultimately sharks get a really bad rap – at the end of the day they’re just another fish, and most of them terrified of humans. The only time I’ve ever felt threatened by sharks is when we’ve been spear-fishing (and having a fish bleed into the water) in areas where larger sharks are known to be common – it’s a calculated risk based on experience and healthy respect for animals that are only acting under instinct in their natural environment. But here’s a few of my more notable experiences with sharks. 8 foot Wobbegong Shark – Off the coast of Perth, October 1997 I’d completed my PADI Junior Open Water Course a few days after my 12th birthday, and my Dad (who’d been my instructor) had taken me out for several dives in the months since. On a few of these post-course dives we’d come across Port Jackson Sharks which were receptive to being held and patted. It might seem surreal to be 12 and holding a shark across your lap, patting it like a dog, but these “sharks” are quite harmless and are regularly found in aquarium touch pools.
And ADORABLE! Port Jackson’s do however have tails that could (if you’re 12) be confused with that of the much  larger and far more aggressive  Wobbegong – a carpet shark that (like Port Jackson’s) sit on the bottom of caves and  will leave a nasty bite. Also, they have teeth that hook backwards preventing them from letting go once they’ve bitten down. So not to be disturbed – for example – by a 12 year old scuba diver that’s seen just it’s tail sticking out of a cave, and tried to pull it out thinking it’s a Port Jackson. The wobby had swum into a cave then stuck his tail out through a hole in the reef. I’ve spotted the tail, thought it was a Port Jackson and tried to pull him out to say hello. And when he didn’t come out, I started pulling harder and harder… Dad turned around to see why I’d fallen behind, only to get the full view – seeing me standing on top of the reef pulling a shark’s tail, and being able to see the massive Wobbegong going mental underneath as I tried to drag a 4 foot wide animal out through a 1 foot hole. Dad started waving frantically to tell me to let go, so I did. The wobby took off like a rocket out from under the reef, straight toward Dad, only to swim right by him and off into the abyss. Needless to say, these days I like to check exactly what I’m dealing with before I go grabbing animal tails underwater. 15 foot Tiger Shark – Gnaraloo Fishing Station, April 2001 Gnaraloo has been an annual pilgrimage for my Dad and his diving/fishing mates since time began – every April they drive 12 hours to get there, sleep in a sheep shed for two weeks fishing and free-diving every day. By my first trip in 2001 I’d already had a bit of experience dealing with sharks while spear-fishing, and within a few days of being in Gnaraloo we’d been chased out of the water by an aggressive hammerhead that started circling and tried to eat the bag full of speared fish. But toward the end of the trip we had about an 8 foot Bronze Whaler (same species and similar size to the shark pictured at the top) swim into where we were spearing. You can usually tell when a shark is around because all the fish start to become scarce. There’s usually a few fish still swimming about though – but this time there eerily everything disappeared. And sure enough, Dad and I saw the Bronze Whaler and started to head back to the boat to move – there’s no point trying to spear fish if a shark is scaring them all away. And that’s when a 15 foot tiger shark loomed out of the darkness and bit the bronze whaler in half.
When the sharks start eating other sharks - YOU NEED TO GET OUT OF THE WATER I’d just like to point out that an 8 foot Bronze Whaler is a formidable shark in itself – while they’re not known to attack humans unprovoked, they do harass spear-fishers – as I’d discovered a month after the wobbegong, when Dad and I had finished a dive (in a place called “Shark Bay” strangely enough) with me latched onto Dad’s back as he was pushing away a circling 6 foot black tip shark with a speargun, while a 10 foot bronze-whaler shark swam around directly underneath us. So watching a tiger shark eat a Bronze-Whaler is enough to a) get you back in the boat in record time, and b) make you wonder if staying in the boat and fishing with a line for the rest of the trip might be a wise move. **Swim with…Whales 90 foot Blue Whale with calf – Ningaloo Reef, May 2008 I’d just started to get back into diving after a few years staying dry, and was about to complete my Divemaster’s qualification when I went on a trip to Exmouth to dive the Ningaloo Reef. We spent a day free-diving with whale sharks, the biggest fish in the world (and not whales). Having an enormous whale shark swim up from the depths straight toward you isn’t an experience you forget easily, and spending a full day swimming with them was amazing. We were incredibly lucky to do that alone. But it wasn’t until the end of the day, when all the boats were heading back to Exmouth, that the whale shark spotter aircraft that was radioing in the locations of the whale sharks spotted the day’s real treat – a blue whale and it’s calf. They were literally going the same way we were on our way back to Exmouth, so the 4 boat contingent maintained a safe distance and took turns individually moving in closer for a slightly better look. We kept our distance, but to say it was huge is an understatement – even the calf was longer than our boat. And while technically we didn’t swim with  the blue whale and it’s calf, I wouldn’t have wanted to. I was lucky enough to see a humpback and a calf a few years later, during a  tech dive on my Extended Range course, that decided to briefly swim by in the distance during a decompression stop. But nothing could compare to spending 15 minutes in a boat next to the largest species of animal ever known to have existed, purely by coincidence of timing. **Swim with…Dolphins Red Sea Dolphins – Eilat, August 17th 1995 In October 1994, Jordan and Israel signed a peace treaty and began opening their borders. My family was living in Aqaba – on the Jordanian side of the Red Sea – when it happened because Dad was working for King Hussein at the time. Being 9 and watching Israeli army engineers clearing land mines live on CNN, and knowing the engineers were the same black dots we could see in the distance from our balcony was a fairly surreal experience. But the opening of the border meant we could visit Eilat – the Israeli city directly opposite Aqaba. It was still pretty difficult to get permission to cross the border into Israel for the day (and Dad couldn’t come with us), so Mum only took my sister and I a couple of times. But the most memorable trip across the border was August 17th 1995, on my 10th birthday, when we visited Dolphin Reef  to swim with the dolphins. I’d paddled with dolphins at Monkey Mia when I was younger, but this was the first experience I’d ever had swimming with them. We spent most of the day there – I was in and out of the water with the dolphins, eating ice cream and drinking Coca Cola (Coke and Pepsi had drawn up battle lines too – you couldn’t buy Coke in Jordan, Pepsi couldn’t be found in Israel) and making the most of a completely surreal birthday.

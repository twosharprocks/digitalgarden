If you read through many of my completed 101 things, you’ll notice I usually put my own slant whatever I’m doing. Something like the all-time greatest films list was as simple as sitting down and watching 132 films. But by then writing 2 line reviews, mixing up a couple of Monty Python’s films & calling Jules et Jim “film noir ”, I managed to do something special by creating a post that causes instant nervous breakdowns in film geeks all over the world. I didn’t need to go to a gun range specifically to complete #43 – I just listed some of the more interesting experiences I’ve had shooting & blowing things up instead.

Now #4 on the 101 Things is “Catch a Fish With Your Bare Hands”, and it’s supposed to be a very calm, zen-like experience. Stand peacefully in some shallow stream, carefully and silently move your hands around the fish without splashing, then gracefully snatch the fish up. Which is pretty much the exact opposite of how I did it.

You’re supposed to be shirtless too, apparently

Likewise, my 2009 trip to Gnarloo Fishing Station with my Dad was supposed to be a photography trip. While Dad and his mates love fishing and I get the whole peaceful zen aspect to it, you’ve probably already guessed that the idea of sitting in a boat somewhere hanging onto a rod & waiting to hook some unsuspecting fish doesn’t hold a lot of appeal to me. But a trip to Gnarloo is a perfect opportunity to practice my underwater photography while capturing images of stunning sea creatures you simply don’t find anywhere else in the world.

A Nudibranch – it’s only about 2cm long

It’s also a great place to get eaten by a shark if you try hard enough. And by “hard enough” I mean “if you’re in the water”. Spear fishing at Gnarloo had gone the way of the Dodo bird a few years previously – partly because the catch-limits are restrictive, but mainly because every time you speared a fish and got blood in the water big-bitey-shark-things would start turning up. One of the few times I did use the speargun up there, the fish I speared was ripped off the spear by a 4 foot reef shark about 30 seconds before his 15 foot Tiger shark mate turned up and chased me out of the water…

As a general rule, we leave them alone and they leave us alone. Sharks are a constant presence in a place like Gnarloo (or further south at the aptly named “Shark Bay”), and rightly so – it’s their environment. But I’ve only ever been forced out of the water by sharks for one of two reasons:

  1. I was covered in fish blood from spear fishing, or 2. The sharks had started eating other sharks AND I was covered in fish blood from spear fishing.

Meet Bruce – he’s a Libra, likes long swims in the ocean and the taste of neoprene-clad free-divers

But generally no fish blood = no problem. You’re still careful though – the black neoprene wetsuits that many free divers are so fond of are easy for a big shark to confuse with a delicious seal. So you try to avoid making too much commotion on the surface, keep an eye out for big-bitey things swimming up underneath you, and you always dive with a buddy so there’s someone to stab and leave as easy pickings while you swim like hell for the boat.

Now on this particular Gnarloo outing, Dad and his mates were keen to remain safe and secure just hanging fishing lines off the back of the boat, while everyone’s favourite ADHD kid wanted to do some free diving to get poorly-lit photos of turtles & stingrays. So I jumped into some of Western Australia’s most hostile waters alone.

Armed only with an oversized camera and a snorkel. Because I am an idiot.

The boat was about 100m away from the nearest reef, with a mix of sandy bottom and seagrass – prime turtle country. Sure enough, paddling toward the reef I managed to get some of the best photos I’ve ever taken of Green Sea turtles, and saw a Loggerhead turtle for the first (and so far only) time.

Taken minutes before it all got WAY too real

Reaching the reef, I noticed all the smaller fish were hiding under rocks and staying out of sight right before I noticed the 4 foot white-tip reef shark hanging around. But it’s not usually that quiet just for a reef shark – something else bigger and more bitey was hanging around in the shadows too. But ADHD kid didn’t think about that very much, noooooo. Because there was gigantic painted crayfish sitting in the first hole I looked into.

Actual size

Now when I say “gigantic”, you need a sense of scale here. Most adult crayfish have bodies about 30cm long, can wrap their legs around something the size of your arm, and you catch them by looping a wire snare around their tail and pulling them gently out of the reef. This monster was over 60cm long with legs over 30cm long. I clipped my camera off to my weight belt, duck-dived down to the crayfish, reached in to pull it out of the hole… and it pulled me in with it.

Planting my feet on the reef either side of monster cray’s home I started fighting it: I would pull it out a few centimetres, it would pull me back a few centimetres. I had the advantage of overall strength, but it had the advantage of being able to breath underwater. I came to the surface gasping a few times, all the while the crayfish dug itself into the hole further. But eventually I wore it out, and wrestled out of its home. Grabbing my bulky camera in one hand, and holding the crayfish held to my chest with the other, and I flicked over onto my back to swim back to the boat.

Which is exactly the time the crayfish started to crawl across my chest to try to latch onto my face and push me underwater.

Just like this, only underwater and made of WAY bigger nightmares (Image Credit – Chapdeus)

So now I’m about 80m away from the boat in open water: $1500 camera that’s heavy enough to pull me underwater on its own in one hand, thrashing around on the surface in a black wetsuit looking like a panicked seal, knowing full well there’s atleast one shark big enough to eat me hanging around, all while using my other hand to fight a monster crayfish that can’t decide if it wants to drown me or eat my face…

The crayfish goes for a third option, deciding my chest can replace the reef as its new home after all, wrapping its gargantuan legs around my back and starting to dig in. I managed to grab its carapace and hold it’s head down against my chest – away from my face – while trying to peddle across the surface and get out of the water before big-bitey-things come to see what all the noise is about.

Once the crayfish was under control and had securely wrapped itself around me, I checked that my camera was still clipped off to… my weight belt WHY AM I STILL WEARING MY WEIGHT BELT WHEN I’M BEING DROWNED BY THE KRAKEN? I glanced down into the water to see that not only was is it now too deep to retrieve my weight belt if I drop it, but as an added bonus an 8-9 foot size bronze whaler (see: “big-bitey-shark-thing”) has decided to follow along as a spectator to my war against crustaceans & drowning.

Suddenly a huge shadow loomed up behind me just as something else brushed the back of my leg. I squealed, then realised I’d swum into the shadow of the boat and kicked the anchor line… I spat out my snorkel, yelled at Dad to take the camera, threw my fins & mask/snorkel into the boat, and scrambled up the ladder with mega-crayfish still latched onto my chest. Then we spent the next 5 minutes trying to get it to detach from my chest, because mega-crayfish had dug into the neoprene of my wetsuit with its legs.

Just before cooking the corpse of my defeated foe

And the worst part I don’t even eat crayfish.

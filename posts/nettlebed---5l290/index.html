About#

Nettlebed Cave is home to a range of interesting features for cave divers to explore. The wall-scratchings which can seen once inside “The Gallery” are probable evidence of megafauna, while entrenched seashells provide insight into the cave’s Ice Age inhabitants.

Beyond The Gallery, the cave drops deeper and breaks into additional rooms containing many large, dark silt-covered boulders, black walls and white-clay floored passages.

The cave finishes in a chamber at 28m, but with an actual penetration distance from the surface of 80m.

Nettlebed Cave is only available for diving on Saturdays and Sundays. A maximum of four divers per day, with one dive each per day, is permitted.

  • LSECR: Page 568 Nettle-Bed Cave was named by cave diver Grant Pearce, after he and his diving partner, Chris Murphy, discovered it on 27 January 1990 after labouring to remove dozens of limestone boulders which choked the south-eastern end of the popular diving doline known as 5L97 (Mud Hole). Grant and Chris’ efforts revealed the presence of a body-sized lake which dropped to an unknown depth under the wall, and their early dives found them exploring two large, virgin submerged chambers which extended for a distance of almost 80 metres from the entrance. The upper sections of the main entry passage were found to harbor unusual wall scratchings to a depth of around 7 metres underwater, and these have subsequently been tentatively identified as being prehistoric megafaunal markings (and perhaps including human petroglyphs) which require urgent protection and further detailed archaeological investigation. Further mapping and research dives were still taking place at this site at the time of this writing.

  • https://www.trimixdivers.com/trimix5/?p=4937: Nettle-Bed Cave (South Australia) was discovered and named by Grant Pearce and Chris Murphy in January 1990. It lies at the opposite end of the doline which contains Mud Hole, in what was until recently a large pine forest.

Early dives required divers to work their ways through the muddy entrance restriction and heaps of limestone rubble to emerge into a wider, higher entrance passage (The Gallery). This has a steep rubbly floor and numerous wall scratchings which were initially suspected as being human in origin, although later studies by specialists reached the conclusion that they were probably caused by now-extinct megafaunal animals. Some old sea-shells were also found in this area of the cave, indicating that Nettle-Bed might well have been used as a habitation site sometime before the last Ice Age ended thousands of years ago.

From The Gallery the cave drops deeper and breaks into a large room which contains many large boulders before dropping again into a low, white clay-floored passage which leads into a smaller final chamber. The cave is not deep by local sinkhole standards – just 28 metres or so in the final room, and with a penetration distance from the surface of some 80 metres – but the restrictive entrance, the delicate wall features and scratchings and the need for divers to possess excellent buoyancy control and near-perfect finning techniques resulted in the feature being rated as a “Penetration” site. The cave has also been listed as a potential site of significance under the Aboriginal Heritage Act (1988).

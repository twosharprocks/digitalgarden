Murra El Elevyn - 6N47

#diving  #caving 

About#

  • A ~300 m main tunnel links to two phreatic networks: one half-filled and one fully flooded. The NE passage (“60 m line”) departs the north side of a large rock; it’s classed Advanced Cave. Locally named Gary’s Back Passage and a “shortcut” from the entrance lake into the main tunnel are also treated as Advanced Cave for training. (cavedivers.com.au)

  • Walls show rich scalloping/phreatic morphologies; photographers consistently report very clear water with red-to-pink limestone tones. (lizrogersphotography.com)

  • The Nullarbor karst here hosts microbial mantles (“snotites”) in several cave lakes including Murra-el-Elevyn—avoid physical contact or wake/silt impacts. (caveconservationaustralia.org)

  • The cave is scientifically notable for bat-guano minerals (type locality for biphosphammite) and palaeo finds reported in literature round-ups (e.g., thylacine remains citations). Treat all deposits as protected. (Mindat)

  • There are instrumented climate datasets (2021-2023) for Murra-el-Elevyn (bihourly T/RH), useful if you want to time imaging for stable conditions/low humidity swings. (doi.pangaea.de)

Potential Leads#

  1. North-West (“60 m line”) continuation This is the cave’s most explicitly “Advanced” line and the obvious vector for continuation. Look for undercut ceilings, rising phreatic tubes, or low-ceiling half-pipes paralleling the main trend. Track scallop asymmetry to confirm paleo-flow and prioritize alcoves on bends.

  2. Interfaces around the “large rock” in the main chamber
    Breakdown piles often mask bypass tubes. Check behind and above the boulder for blind domes or tube lips; inspect for faint percolation staining or clean, eddy-scoured floor patches indicating through-draft water movement.

  3. “Gary’s Back Passage” and the entrance-to-main-tunnel shortcut Both are named as Advanced segments—good flags that they’re tighter/less travelled. These are prime spots to find side tubes that were skipped when the main line was established. Slow, close wall-scans often reveal inset phreatic notches.

  4. Joint-controlled leads aligned with regional structure Nullarbor caves commonly exploit joint sets; historical mapping notes exist for Murrawijinie (SA) showing joint frequency plots—useful analogs for what to look for in plan view at Murra-el-Elevyn: straight runs, right-angle turns, bead-string chambers.

  5. Ceiling domes & cupolas in the half-filled network
    In mixed air/water sections, inspect cupolas for high-level feeders. Dry-tooling to a visible rim may reveal a tubes’ continuation once submerged a few metres farther on.

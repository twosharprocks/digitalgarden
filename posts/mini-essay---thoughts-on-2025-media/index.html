Kindle#

Not Fade Away (Jim Dodge) I have to laugh about this book because it’s on the 101 Things book list, but I accidentally read all of “Not Fade Away” by Ben Torres-Fong (about the early years of Rolling Stone) and didn’t realise I’d read the wrong book until I went to tick it off the list. I enjoyed both, but this one was certainly a lot weirder and more entertaining. It took me awhile to figure out that the journey was just an excuse to meet weird and wonderful characters along the way, but once I recognised that it was easy to settle in and see wherever the author decided to take it. Reading it wasn’t life changing, but it was certainly a fun read.

Walden (Henry Thoreau) Errrgh, this fucking book. I read this back in 2019 after hearing it referenced in the finale of “Parks an Recreation”, and had high hopes it’d be an inspiring and engaging perspective on life among nature, and specifically the dense pine forests and stunning lakes of the US. I forced my way through this book the first time in 2019, finishing it primarily because it was an audio book I could tune in and out of while walking the dog. Having rewatched “Parks and Recs” again recently, I was similarly inspired to pick up Walden again, this time as an ebook… And felt near instant regret. I’m clearly missing the magic that other people find when they read this, but I genuinely can’t tolerate even a few pages of Thoreau’s prose - all I can think whenever I try is how insufferable I’d have found him in person if we ever met.

Audible#

The Holographic Universe (Michael Talbot) This was the first book I finished in 2025, and I’ll admit my memory of it is already hazy. What I do remember is that I resonated closely with much of what it shared, and it pushed me to look more closely at holographic theory and consider how it might align with the other reading I’d been doing around quantum information theory too. It also reminded me that Gerard T’Hooft - who I knew from his involvement with Mars One - had been one of world leaders in holographic theory, and how it presents a unique and deeply appealing perspective on the nature of the universe. This is certainly a book I should (and want to) read again in 2026.

A Life of Meaning (James Hollins) This is another book that I read early in the year and don’t have clear memories of. I do know that I enjoyed it, and it felt like quite a gentle read - there wasn’t anything groundbreaking in it for me, but it wasn’t in any way boring or pandering. Like the Holographic Universe, this is something I should probably read again in 2026.

Being Ram Dass (Ram Dass) My first introduction to Ram Dass was through “The Midnight Gospel” on Netflix, with the classic “Just Be Here Now” quote ending the series. I’ve been intrigued for awhile by Alan Watts, and I felt a similar strain of impish spiritualism while listening to Ram Dass’s lectures in “Becoming Nobody” last year, so I picked up “Being Ram Dass” as a way to learn more about Richard Alprin and later Ram Dass. Understanding the journey he took and the person he evolved into as he delvd deeper into love was fascinating, and in many ways this book was more important to read and understand than just his lectures in “Becoming Nobody”. His book “Be Here Now” is in my kindle too, and I expect that it’ll be one of the first things I read in 2026 once I’ve finished the books left from 101 Things - To Do.

Showing Up (Nedd Brockmann) All I can say about this and Nedd Brockmann is what an absolute legend. Some people know they love pushing themselves to do remarkable things, and then there are folks like Nedd who go out and do remarkable things over and over and over again. Not only does Nedd share what it was like to run such epic distances day-in and day-out, but it’s clear throughout the book that he’s just a genuinely good guy who’s doing what he’s doing as a way of supporting a cause he believes in. A genuine pleasure to listen to, and finishing this I felt absolutely inspired to go out and do more remarkable things with my life too.

The Anthropocene Reviewed (John Green) I genuinely don’t remember listening to this. Goodreads tells me I rated it 4 stars, and I remember being excited to read something from John Green who I’d first seen a quote from through Zen Pencils. But I just don’t remember anything from this. Maybe I should relisten to it in 2026, but my gut tells me I wouldn’t get a lot out of it from a 2nd listen.

Can’t Hurt Me (David Goggins) After the way Nedd Brock man raved about meeting Dave Goggins at the end of his book, I got really excited and decided to look him up… And realised that “Can’t Hurt Me” was the same book that Auible had been recommending every time I opened it for the previous few months! This couldn’t have been more of a disappointment compared to Nedd’s book though. I have all the respect in the world for what Dave Goggins did getting through some of the toughest military training in the world, but I also really struggled with the knuckle-dragging mentality. He’s an incredible example that we are ALL capable of when we truly commit body and soul, but I also have to wonder what it all proved. I’m glad I read this, but I’m not sure what I learned from it.

Horus Heresy Series - Warhammer 40k There was a brief period at the start of 2025 when I was looking at getting into painting WH40k models again - something I’d done in high-school, but had dropped in uni and hadn’t been in one place long enough to consider again since. The interest soon passed when I remembered how expensive the plastic is (genuinely eye-watering) but I did go through a run of Games Workshop audiobooks from the Warhammer 40k universe, starting with the Horus Heresy trilogy (“Horus Rising”, “False Gods”, and “Galaxy in Flames”). Some of this was really well written sci-fi, some of it was a bit average, but all of it was interesting from a lore perspective. Having never really understood the WH40k universe, it was interesting to start from the point where it all went wrong (and got interesting) with the fall of Warmaster Horus and the corruption of half the space marine chapters. My limited interest in the boardgames was always massively overshadowed by the lore, and these three books broke down the heart of why the imperium of man looked the way it does in the year 40,000. That said, there were always more interesting parts of the space Marines than the core loyalist chapters and those that turned to chaos, and I realise not that I have always been most intrigued by the edge cases, which explains why I finished this trilogy and moved onto books focused on Alpha Legion and the Dark Angels

Legion (Dan Abnett) - Warhammer 40k This was honestly one of the best books I read out of the entire “Black Library”. Dan Abnett is undoubtably one of the best writers Games Workshop works with, and Alpha Legion are easily one of the most intriguing (chaos?) space marine chapters around. This reads as much like sci-fi as it does a spy novel, and is one of the few WH40k books that I’d recommend as a complete stand-alone or to someone who doesn’t know anything about the universe.

Dark Angels Series - Warhammer 40k Right from the time I first started getting interested in Warhammer in high school, the Dark Angels were what hooked me… And it was honestly just the green colour scheme. Like Alpha legion though, I was lucky that my interest was tied to a chapter that had such a rich and fascinating lore. In high school I only got exposed to that via the 4th edition Dark Angels Codex, but 2025 was the year I discovered that there was an extensive collection of well-made Dark Angel audiobooks on Audbile that covered the history of Caliban before the Lion El Johnson even arrived, all the way through to the total destruction of the planet itself. “Descent of Angels”, “Fallen Angels” and “Angels of Caliban” kind of blend together now that I look back on them, but none of them were disappointing - the whole lore around the corruption of the planet, the betrayal of the Fallen, and the shame that the chapter carries all make for exceptional reading. While I wouldn’t go back to read the Horus Heresy again, I’d be more than temoted to revisit these three.

A Thousand Sons - Graham McNeill I don’t get why the Thousand Sons interested me in WH40k. They’re fully fledged chaos marines, use spells, have a weird Ancient Egyptian vibe and are relatively fragile in-game - all things I wanted to avoid. And yet their lore has always fascinated me, and so I found myself listening to the book dedicated to their Horus Heresy era turn to chaos. It’s a sign of a good Warhammer 40k book when you know how it all ends, but you’re still hoping the kind-of bad guys don’t get absolutely annihilated by the dickish “good guys” (the Space Wolves).

The Master of Mankind - Aaron Dembski-Bowden After all the talk about the Emperor throughout all the Horus Heresy era books I’d read, I figured I"d jump ahead to the book dedicated to him. Honestly, this guy is pretty scummy, but I guess that’s why he makes a great character. The main thing I remember from reading this was hearing about all the lore from the battle for Terra, when the Emperor was just the leader of some war band killing other war bands in the wasteland of Earth while doing weird genetic experiments with his own geneseed. What was more interesting than the Emperor himself was the pretorians that were fighting to protect him. Having never heard of them before, it was fascinating to hear the perspective from the characters tasted with protecting the God-Emperor of humanity.

The Teachings of Don Juan - Carlos Castenada Total change of gears from far-future fantasy sci-fi to Carlos Castenada doing peyote in the desert. I’d heard all about the incredible writing of Castenada and even caught the name drop in “Cloud Atlas” when Tom Hanks’ character asks Halle Berry’s if she ever read any of it. I’d assumed that reading this would itself be some sort of perspective-altering experience, but honestly it just reads like some gringo going down to Mexico and doing a bunch of drugs with an old guy who spoke in riddles. I"m sure this was revolutionary when it was published in the 60’s (?) but when Audible recommended the sequel (included for free in my membership) I decided to pass.

The Tao of Pooh - Benjamin Hoff (reread) I first listened to this driving back from a trip to Olwogin cave on the Roe Plains a few years back, and it was exactly what I needed at the time when I was trying to do too many things and (in the words of Pooh) I was being a “Bissy Backson”. Listening to this on the drive back from the Nullarbor felt just as timely, as I’d slipped into my old “Bissy” ways again and had left my job a few weeks earlier so I could put more time and energy into things I actually care about rather than rushing around trying to do everything and please everyone. I haven’t heard particularly good things about the follow up “The Te of Piglet” but I can absolutely endorse the Tao of Pooh as something I’m going to keep coming back to whenever I lose sight of what’s important in life.

You Are Not So Smart - David McRaney This was probably my least favourite read of 2025. I’m not sure what I thought I was getting when I bought a book on psychology that literally talks down to you with its title, but the whole thing felt incredibly condescending from start to finish. Nothing in this felt particularly revolutionary from a psychology perspective, and all of it felt smug and dickish as it built emotional strawmen with hypothetical situations describing how “you” think something might happen, but then says (every fucking chapter) “but you are not so smart” before explaining why you’re a moron for even thinking the way it’s described. Hard pass, 0 stars.

Hour of the Wolf - Blaine Lee Pardoe Much of 2024"s light audiobook listening was consumed with Battletech novels of wildly ranging quality, so when I saw Blaine Lee Pardoe had written another one about the very end of the war for the Inner Sphere with Clan Wolf’s race to claim Terra, then I figured I’d add one more Battletech book to my library. Blaine Lee Pardoe is a bit of a controversial author too given his right-wing Trump-sequel politics (and his history of having some of the characters spout those politics in monologues) but I think he had a very short leash for this novel - given this is book is essentially the end of the Battletech timeline, and the characters in it have been well established in other previous books, there probably wasn’t a lot of room for the Khan of Clan Wolf to suddenly start spouting about libertariaism. But after over 100 Battletech books published through the decades, it was great to read the finale to an extraordinary and fascinating universe.

“Endymion” & “The Rise of Endymion” - Dan Simmons These two books bleed together, but they’re also strangely disconnected from the first two books in the Hyperion saga. If you’ve not read Hyperion, then you absolutely should, but I’m also glad there was a significant gap between my reading of book two (“Fall of Hyperion”) and these books (books three and four). This is twisty, dense and confusing sci-fi at its best, so if you read Hyperion and didn’t like or get it, then don’t push on hoping it gets better - you’ll either like Dan Simmons’ work or you won’t.

“Foundation and Empire” & “Second Foundation” - Isaac Asimov Speaking of twisty, dense and confusing sci-fi, I also finished the Foundation series with these two books. This was definitely spurred on by the excellent TV series, and it was fascinating to read these prior to season three which introduced “The Mule” for the first time. The TV adaptation is excellent given the age of the source material, but that’s not a criticism of these books either - merely that they were certainly written many decades ago, and that occasionally shows in the writing.

Quit - Annie Duke Having never heard of Annie Duke before, I’m not sure why this popped up on my radar to read… other than I was leaving my office job at the same time. Whatever the reason, I’m glad I did - I went into this thinking it’d be a slightly bland take on why people should leave shitty jobs sooner, and certainy wasn’t expecting to hear from a highly-successful professional poker player talking about all the biases humans have towards holding on to everything (jobs, relationships, possessions) for far too long. We invest and then become predisposed to keeping that investment well beyond it’s obvious use-by date, when the logical thing to do is to let go and evolve. I’ll almost certainly reread this in 2026, and hopefully the lessons it provided will sink in a little deeper than they did the first time.

Slow Down - Constance Kassor While “Quit” was a wonderful surprise to read, “Slow Down” felt like a chore. Maybe I came into it with the expectation that it’d provide some insights on how to slow down in life (something I’ve been claiming to want for years now) but what I got was a fairly self-indulgent description of one woman’s year long sabbatical. I kept thinking there’d be some sort of insight or wisdom that’d help others slow down and find a more enjoyable pace in life. But that moment never came, and based on the reviews on Goodreads I’m certainly not the only one who put this book down disappointed.

Blake Crouch Collection: Dark Matter, Recursion, Upgrade - Blake Crouch When I trialled AppleTV, the Dark Matter TV series was one of the first things recommended to me, but I instinctively pushed back against it - I’d signed up for Silo and Foundation, not for some odd split-reality series featuring Joel Edgerton. The audiobook must have popped up on special though, and as soon as I started listening I was hooked. All three books put genuinely edge science (the many-worlds hypothesis, the proposed quantum nature of conciousness, and viral transhumanism) front-and-centre, and push the concepts to the very edge while blending them with insanely-fast action. It’s genuinely hard to keep up with these books, but that challenge is half of the joy - you’re trying to think as hard as the protagonists to figure out the next step. As a trained physicist with an interest in the nature of conciousness, the first two (Dark Matter and Recursion) were easily the most interesting, but Upgrade forced me well outside my comfort zone learning more about human biology than I ever have beforeand. All three books are exceptional, and well worth the effort for anyone interested in modern hard sci-fi… And I did watch (and enjoy) the Dark Matter TV series once I’d finished all three books.

The Dalai Lama’s Cat, Books 4-6 - David Michie I’ve always had a soft spot for David Michie’s books ever since my Mum met him and gave me a paperback version of The Magician of Lhasa. These books are so easy and engaging it’s often easy to mix up which ones you’ve read, which is why I reread For Paws of Spiritual Success before moving onto Awaken the Kitten Within and realising I’d already read that previously too. not that it mattered - it’s a joy to listen to any of these books, and having the author narrate them just adds to the experience. Like the five books before it, Claws of Attraction perfectly wraps a series of core Buddhist tenants in a gentle but engaging story all told through the eyes of “H.H.C” aka “His Holiness’s Cat”. Reading them in order adds a little bit of overall story context, but honestly you could pick any of them up in any order and have a wonderful experience learning a little more about Buddhism from the “most beautiful creature who ever lived”

Altered Carbon Trilogy - Richard K Morgan Netflix’s Altered Carbon was one of the TV series that got me through 2020. So while I loved watching both seasons at the time, I also remembered very little about them when I saw the audio books on sale in 2025. Everything came flooding back as soon as I started listening to the first book though, which I quickly realised had been followed almost scene-for-scene by the first season of the TV series. Where things got very interesting though was listening to books 2 and 3 (Broken Angels and Woken Furies). I’d read a lot of complaints online about the 2nd season of the Netflix series, and watching it in 2020 I hadn’t really understood what the problem was… But reading the trilogy and then rewatching the 2nd season made it abundantly clear that Netflix had carved up both books and botched them both over 8 episodes. Broken Angels shifts completely away from the first book’s police procedural approach and into an incredible war story - putting Takeshi Kovacs in his absolute element, while developing more of the alien “Elder” mythology. Woken Furies takes that even further by recovering Quillcrest Falconer and putting an Elder in the same mind, and setting the whole thing on Takeshi’s home planet of Harlans World. These books are extraordinary (while also being pretty gratuitous with both sex and violence) but it’s pretty clear Netflix tried to take the best of both without allowing the space to develop characters or plot properly. Having the Netflix series end with the door left open for another season felt disingenuous too - these books are great reads and we’ll worth your time, so the biggest gripe is that the 2nd season of the TV series didn’t do either of them any justice.

The Combat Doctor - Dan Pronk This is one of those books that Audbile recommended to me for a low time before I finally decided to use a credit to see what the fuss was about… And I’m glad I did. Dr Dan Pronk served with Australian special forces in Afghanistan around the same time I was training with the British Royal Marines, and much of what he describes reflects what I was seeing of life

Cryptonomicon - Neal Stephenson

Bhagavad Gita - Anonymous This is one of those religious texts that I’d always been interested in reading for general understanding, but also wanted an objective interpretation of the text and didn’t really know where to start in finding one. In the end it was as easy as seeing a highly rated audio book version and buying it. This is a relatively short book (a long poem if we’re being exact) and I listened to all of it in a single sitting, and the translator’s introduction and recap at the end really helped set the initial context and to clarify the important details. I have a kindle copy of this too, but I’m not sure I’ll ever read it - the audibook version was perfect, and allowed me to dip in and out of parts I might not have followed or pushed through but which got the perfect explanation at the end in the recap.

Wayne Dyer’s Ultimate Library - Wayne Dyer This is a behemoth of an audio book, and it pulls snippets from Wayer Dyer’s lectures across more than 30 years. An old friend I’ve lost touch with (and who I sadly suspect might have passed away) used to love listening to Wayne Dyer lectures, and so listening to this allowed me to reconnect to them in a way. There’s no doubt that Wayne Dyer represented a beautiful and clear view on spirituality and the way we interact with each other and the universe, and this was quickly added to my Audible favourites so it’s ready for a future relisten.

Starship Troopers - Robert Heinlein My first exposure to Starship Troopers was the 1997 film, which could not be more different from the book itself, but both of them continue to be fascinating pieces of literature. I first read the Starhip Troopers novel by Robert Heinlein when I was bumping in and out of hostels in London after passing the PRMC to join the Royal Marines in mid 2009, but prior to starting training with them towards the end of 2009. Revisiting it 16 years later, it’s all the more obvious that Heinlein’s perspective on war is much more complex than many commentators gave him credit for. The Verhoven film remains a masterclass in satire, but the novel feels like someone who had experienced war trying to come to grips with both it’s abject horror and the elation of being part of a larger mission (and how easily that can be hijacked and twisted into fascism). This is still a remarkable read (and relatively short) but it certainly didn’t have the impact this time that it did when I read it before starting training with the RM - I’m a different person doing different things with a radically different perspective on warfare after leaving the military behind a decade and a half ago.

Agent to the Stars - John Scalzi John Scalzi has quickly become a regular sci-fi favourite of mine ever since I read “The Kaji Appreciation Society” a few years ago. This kept being recommended in Audible, and I was looking for something to break up the mammoth Wayne Dyer I’d just finished. If anything this was more about the way Hollywood works than it was about aliens, but like so much of John Scalzi’s novels this was all about taking a central sci-fi idea and letting the different characters interact and play around it. Some of the plot points towards the end felt very obvious, but that didn’t stop it from being a really engaging and funny read - if there’s one thing I love about John Scalzi books is that even the serious ones are packed with humour, and this one was particularly funny from start to finish.

I’m Glad My Mom Died - Jennette McCurdy I went into this book with absolutely no idea who Jeanette McCurdy was or why she’d written a book about how she was glad her mother was dead - I picked it up purely because it’d been recommended by Audible and I figured she was a comedian who’d written a satirical book with her Mom’s death as a dark punchline. What I really didn’t expect was to read a book by a world-famous child actor who was bared her soul to share the way how she had been physically, emotionally, and sexually abused by her deeply troubled mother in pursuit of stardom in Hollywood. This book completely sideswiped me, and for the longest time I considered trying to contact Jeanette to thank her for writing something so unbelievably raw and funny. I’m certain if I had written to her it would have been lost in the torrent of messages I’m sure she receives from her iCarly fans, but I genuinely knew nothing about iCarly going into this book - I’m just in awe that someone could write something so honest that was simultaneously horrifying and hilarious. I never thought I’d say this about a memoir from someone most known for a Disney kids show I knew nothing about, but reading “I’m Glad My Mom Died” simultaneously made me want to be a better writer and to email an author I’d never heard of before to simply say thank you. So Jeanette if you ever read this - thank you, and please never stop writing.

Redshirts - John Scalzi This is easily the best example I’ve read of John Scalzi’s style as he takes a sci-fi idea and stretches it as far as he can. There are a couple of interesting plotting elements that were added early on that took most of the book to fully come to grips with, UT for the most part this was a very fun and meta reading experience. I broke this audiobook up with “Fahrenheit-182” by Mark Hoppus as I was driving out to the Nullarbor on a cave diving expedition, switching between them one chapter at a time, and it was always tempting to sneak an extra chapter of Redshirts whenever John Scalzi left the chapter on a cliffhanger.

Farenheit-182 - Mark Hoppus My exposure to Blink-182 started with a friend’s copy of “Dude Ranch”, and I’ll even admit to hunting down a copy of “Cheshire Cat” in my final year of high school, but like many people I became a real fan when “Enema of the State” was released. It’s the only album that I’ve ever had my Mum suggest I keep somewhere else - not because of the lyrics, but because the front cover is a stripper in a busty nurse costume who’s seductively/threatengly pulling on a rubber glove, and 15 year old Josh genuinely didn’t realise it might be a problem. All of that to say that Mark Hoppus’s memoir gave me a far greater appreciation for who Blink-182 is and why they’ve one through the various evolutions they have over the last 25 years I’ve listened to their music. The sheer grind of there early touring was inspiring, and something that I guess many rock & punk bands have to do in the early days, but the utter punishment of 3 dudes living in a van, driving through the night to get to the next venue, and counting penny’s to buy a McDonald’s breakfast each morning, all so they could play for 20 minutes in some dive bar - that level of commitment is both inspiring and crazy, and it reminded me of the desperate determination I saw in comics trying to break through in the UK and the lengths they’d go to because they believed deep down they simply HAD to perform. I also didn’t realise that Mark Hoppus had undergone an absolutely brutal chemotherapy treatment in 2020, and it’s nice to know that after all the ugly shit that had happened within the band through the years that Tom Delonge was the first person on the phone when the news of Mark’s treatments broke. This was a generally wonderful read, although I’ll admit that relistening to the albums as I was reading the book wasn’t as fun as I’d hoped - it’s pretty clear my interest in Blink-182’s music hasn’t moved much since I was in high school, so while it was awesome to hear Dude Ranch and Enema of the State again, and parts of California too, there was zero nostalgia for Self-Titled, Neighbourhoods, Eight, or the recent albums when Tom returned.

Wanderlust - Reid Mitenbuler I’d been inadvertently paying for audiobook access through Spotify for awhile, so when I realised I cancelled the subscription but still wanted to make the most of the remaining month while I had it - “Wanderlust” popped up as a recommendation and I’m really glad it did. This audiobook was what I listened to each morning as I started each day of my expedition to the Nullarbor, and there was a strange juxtaposition of listening to the biography of one of the main characters in the Heroic Age of Arctic exploration while cooking breakfast before climbing down a hole in the desert to discover and map new underwater passages. Knowing there’s a painting of Peter Fryken in the Explorer’s Club in NYC makes me want to visit all the more to appreciate this unique and big-hearted character who discovered more of the world while making it better by being in it - I wish I’d had the chance to meet him, but this book is the next best thing.

Eon - Greg Bear This book was an absolute trip for so many different reasons, the biggest because I kept being confused about when it had been written. While it’s at it’s heart a pretty mind-bending sci-fi story about an artificial space created by future humans which accidentally loops back to a critical time in Earth’s history, the heart of the story is set in the 2020’s (eg. Now) and the yet it delves into all sorts of mindsets and geopolitics that feel more at home in the 1970’s and 80’s (specifically the US’s relations with the Soviet Union and China). The audiobook was also published in 2020 which makes reading it in 2025 feel like it was intended as an alternate history novel. It was only when I realised the original book had been written in the 1980s that I realised that it had been written as genuine sci-fi and not alternate history, and Greg Bear wasn’t trying to do some some of interesting “What If this happened instead” story from the context of the 2020’s but a “What if this happens in the future” based in the politics and attitudes of the 1980s. I won’t say it’s particularly well-written, but as a concept the book is pretty incredible and highlights the cyclic nature of time and reality that I think we’re still struggling with as a species.

Down with the System - Serj Tankian System of a Down has always been one of those bans that I grew up obsessing over at different times in my life but then just as quickly forgot about. I’d always been interested in Serj Tankian’s story though, so when I saw he’d written a memoir I figured it was time to understand more about the guy who I’d always assumed was a pretty typical rocker who’d been the primary creative force behind SOAD and had used the platform to discuss the Armenian genocide. What I hadn’t realised was that Derren had always been the musical heart of the band, and that Serj was really the vocal artist who’d been adding the flourishes and quirks to what Derren was really directing. I also didn’t realise that the band hadn’t put out an album in nearly two decades, or that there had been so much in-fighting. Reading Serj’s memoir, it also seems like he didn’t realise he’d pissed the others so much. True to Serj’s style though, he’s respectfully honest and vulnerable about the band’s challenges and holds absolutely no bitterness towards his band brothers, even when the Trump-supporting politics of John (SOAD’s drummer and Serj’s brother-in-law) are so existentially-opposed to Serj’s own left leaning tendencies.

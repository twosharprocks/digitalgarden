Long term readers will know I’ve been on a challenge for the last few years to complete Richard Horne’s “101 Things To Do Before You Die” list. And while a lot of the 101 things are epic life-changing events, many of them don’t deserve a complete College of Awesome post. As a result I’m put putting together a compilation series of the 101 things I’ve completed…

…starting right now.

6 – Throw a House Party When Your Parents Are Out
Perth, Australia – New Years Eve 2004/2005

Around Christmas 2004, a business associate of my parents – for reasons that to this day remain a mystery to all of us – asked if I would house-sit and care for their dog while they were away on holiday. The business associate is one of the most severely anally retentive human beings I have ever met, and someone who prided herself on keeping her house at a “Display Home” level of cleanliness. There were sections of the house I couldn’t go into, glassware I couldn’t use, and areas of carpet I couldn’t walk on. The dog used to get nervous around strange people and loud noises, and would wet itself when things got too stressful.

Now I’m not sure if she’d had a serious car accident that day and was suffering from a major concussion or if she’d just licked some lead glass windows washed with LSD, but something major had obviously happened for her to ask me to care for her house and pet. Because she clearly had no idea who she was trusting.

Technically New Year’s Eve wasn’t a house party when my parents were out of the ourhouse. But I still feel I’ve completed this 101 thing, and made up for it by being trusted with the house of a woman important to my parent’s business, and making sure by the next morning it looked like a warzone. To simplify the night’s highlights;

  • A fight with some random hoody kids nearly started on the street, when one of my socially-inept uni mates inexplicably decided to call a group of them retarded.
  • Atleast one person relieved themselves in a flower pot, right next to the antique furniture that had migrated from the “banned carpet area” out to the back patio.
  • The police responded to a noise complaint, arriving about 10 minutes after the hoody kids had come back and thrown a carton of milk at the house
  • Someone vomited in the laundry (which initially I’d locked), and one of the girls tried to put a tea towel on the dog as a saddle and ride it like a horse.

Needless to say we wound up with a broken window, a variety of neighbour complaints, and my parents business associate has refused to make eye contact with me ever since.

50 – Write your name over a star on the walk of fame
Hollywood, U.S – Jan 4th 2006

I’m not proud of it, but I have to admit: I used to watch Home Improvement. I was young and ignorant, but it was still the pinnacle of cookie-cutter sitcom comedy, so god knows what the appeal was. Maybe it was the creepy weirdo they had peering over their fence all the time, maybe it was Al’s sarcastic ridicule of the “star”, or maybe it was that Heidi the tool-time girl helped put me through puberty.

What I sure as hell wasn’t watching for was this guy;

The real question is which one is the bigger tool?

Voicing Buzz Lightyear and being in Galaxy Quest are the only reasons no one hasn’t fired Tim Allen into the sun yet. And I’m certain Galaxy Quest’s appeal was boosted significantly by the combination of Alan Rickman’s velvety voice and Sigourney Weaver’s lycra-defying breasts. So imagine my surprise when I found out the master of “Men and women are different” stand-up had been awarded a star on the Hollywood Walk of Fame for his “contributions” to “comedy”.

86 – Run a Marathon

Sometimes you start to take life a little too seriously, and actually start to grow emotionally attached to people. Apparently spending 8 months training to be Royal Marine can do that – it also gets you pretty physically fit. I left training and a lot of really good mates in June 2010, because contrary to popular belief I’d actually prefer to rant/scream/dance around on a stage with puppets than go to exotic locations, live in a tent and shoot people for a living. If you feel like I’ve lied to you all these years and would like to stop reading, I will completely understand…

By May I was settling into a job in the English country-side, riding my bike to and from work and staying pretty healthy moving things around at work each day. A month before, most of the guys I’d trained with had deployed on a 6-month tour to Afghanistan, when the inevitable happened: the BBC reported a Royal Marine had been killed in an IED attack, but his name hadn’t been released yet. They did report he’d been killed in the part of Helmand province I knew most of my mates were operating, so the waiting game started. I’d been out doing some serious drinking the night before with my new workmates, so I was already feeling pretty sorry for myself. And as you could probably guess, waiting is not one of my strong points. Unless it’s waiting for revenge, ofcourse – then I can (and have) waited literally years.

So the only thing I could think of was to go for a run – get the blood flowing, sweat out the alcohol and clear my head. I put on my gear, started the GPS tracker and my “Hard Run” playlist, and started running. And kept running.

And running. And running…

90 minutes later the playlist looped back to the first track, and I checked the odometer for the first time since I’d started: I’d just covered 21km (13 miles) – half a marathon – and had only just stopped worrying that it was the bloke who’d missed the birth of his first child by a couple of weeks. I also realised I was 21km away from home, out on an isolated country road as it was getting dark. So I turned around and started to run back. And on the way home, my body decided to remind me that we aren’t really designed to run more than about 30km in a single go: I hit the wall just over the 32km mark, with still another 10 km to get home, and everything seized. Supposedly marathon training is about building glycogen stores so hitting the wall isn’t so dramatic, but since I hadn’t really trained for my impromptu marathon that wasn’t much help. I couldn’t vomit because my stomach muscles had locked up, and my thighs were screaming out to the point I had to run in a baby-step shuffle.

When I got home I was a scraping, gibbering mess. But I have to admit a guilty sense of relief, that while a young Royal Marine did tragically pass away it wasn’t someone I knew.

So in the end I ran the 42.2 km (26.1 miles) in 3 hours, 53 minutes and 8 seconds – hardly a breath-taking time for a trained marathon runner, but not too bad for someone with a hangover that left the house not knowing he was about to run a marathon.

Michelle Smith – Australia’s only jazz harpist

Some people are born musicians. The first time they picked up a recorder in primary school, a majestic cacophony of melody poured out that stunned their teachers & peers. When they sit down at a piano or pick up a guitar, every note is loaded with harmony, every strum filled with emotion and meaning. Michelle Smith (pictured above) is one of these people. They shift effortlessly between different notes, chords and keys, as if they’re simply switching in time to the ebb and flow of different resonate frequencies in the universe.

I’m not one of those people.

The first time I had to learn a musical instrument in school I was 10 – two years behind every other kid in the Western Australian education system. My family and I had just returned from living in Jordan for two years, and learning to play an instrument wasn’t part of the distance education curriculum at the time. So I returned to regular schooling to discover that not only did I have a sociopath for a year 6 teacher, but I was expected to already be a reasonably accomplished recorder player having never seen one of those horrid tubes of despair before in my life.

So I sucked at it. And I naturally hated class music lessons.

My school didn’t give up though. At the start of Year 7 we were all given a musical aptitude test – I failed it. But somehow they decided that I’d scored just high enough to learn the double bass. In reality I think my school just thought it would be comical to watch a kid who was barely 4 foot tall carry home a 7 foot bass each day, and they were probably right.

But as you’d expect, I sucked at it.

This didn’t stop me from falling in love with listening to music though. While I’d been a slow starter, my first high school crush introduced me to Merrick & Rosso on Triple J, and my life was altered forever. Next thing I’m tuning in every morning & evening, expounding the virtues of non-commercial radio to people in the street, and trying to impress girls that I knew whatever the latest underground indie rock band was too, like some sort of awkward pubescent wannabe-hipster. Actually looking back now I’m not sure if I was in love with the music, the crush who introduced me to it all, or Merrick & Rosso’s roving reporter at the time – the very lovely Myf Warhurst.

Lets be honest – it was mostly Myf

All I knew for sure was that I couldn’t get enough of the radio, and was soon amassing a hefty collection of alternative rock albums. So I knew I loved music, but I sucked at playing it myself. I tried bass guitar for a while, then somehow wound up playing in a Christian rock band through Year 11 & 12. Which was pretty awkward since I was also a vocal and hard-line atheist at the time… I hit university, sold my bass guitar & bought more albums, dive bombing even further into appreciating music and completely forgot about playing music for over 8 years.

Less bass, more bass girls…

Fast forward to Christmas 2010 and I’m breaking off a year-long relationship with a primary school music teacher, who in horrifying retrospect bares a striking resemblance both physically & emotionally to my old Year 6 teacher. I’ve packed my bags and left for the icy streets of London three days before Christmas, preferring to chance it and not know where I’ll sleep each night rather than deal with any more of the crazy. And a week later she sends me a message asking to “patch things up and give (me) a Christmas present”. We talk, resolve to try to remain friends, and she gives me a £20 ukulele. I didn’t even touch it.

Because I probably would have done this to it. (Image Credit: Oddsock)

After 4 months of bouncing in & out of bed bug-ridden hostels through London (during which I wrote my first solo show “Apocalypse Meow” rather aptly about having your world implode) I finally managed to land a job as a technician & science advisor to Damien Hirst way out in Gloucestershire. The teacher and I were still trying to be friends at this stage, but my tolerance for her bat-shit-crazy episodes was starting to drop at the same time – you only put up with people who treat you slightly better than you treat yourself, and I was discovering life was a hell of a lot happier out of the military and away from her.

It wasn’t until she came out to visit me in Stroud – where she threw a hissy fit and I abandoned her in a field to be eaten by wolves – that I decided to actually learn to play the ukulele she’d given me instead of glaring at it in the corner like the ghost of relationships-past. And the very first thing I learnt was the opening riff to Duelling Banjos, then sent a video of me playing it to some people in Perth …

It wasn’t until I realised the comedy potential of electronically heckling people from high school for getting married to each others sisters – by playing a guitar made for hobbits – that I started to really love playing music and not just listen to it.

Suddenly it was easy to play along to your favourite songs, and then laugh at how ridiculous (or awesome) they sound on ukulele. I went from someone who came out in a rash at the idea of learning to play music to buying new strings to improve the tone, within a week. Within a month I was writing my own songs, and just 2 months after that I spent the day walking all over Bristol with John Robertson to find a new semi-acoustic tenor ukulele – the ukulele I would use in my début solo show at the Edinburgh Fringe festival: Apocalypse Meow.

It’s also where Gerald the Baboon would threaten to rub baby oil into bald men’s scalps at every show…

Now the first thing you need to do when you buy an amazing instrument, is name it. It took me a month before finding a name that even remotely fit. After I huge day at work I sat out on the back patio for a few hours, looking out over Stroud and writing songs on my new ukulele ahead of the festival. I realised was shattered but was only a few verses off finishing a song, so I left the ukulele on the patio couch and wandered inside to make a coffee. Walking back outside I glanced up and just about dropped my cup – for half a second the cushions & ukulele had combined to look like one of the girls from work was sleeping on my the couch.

Cherry had been over a few weeks before and we’d sat on the same couch for dinner, which seemed to be enough inspiration for a ukulele name & a full blown visual hallucination. “Cherry” didn’t really seem to suit this uke, although “Sheree” fit a little better. But after Edinburgh 2011 the name “Sheree” stopped seeming to fit too – a lot of weird things had gone down at the festival, Cherry and I had stopped hanging out, and I felt like a different person. And the tone of the ukulele seemed to reflect the confusion: she developed a buzz, and all the strings started to sound muted & flat.

A friend from the 2010 fringe got back in touch just as the festival finished though: one of those sign-post gingers I’ve mentioned, Amanda. And weirdly enough when I tried calling my ukulele “Amanda” in the December, the buzzing stopped and the life in that beautiful varnished wooden box seemed to spring right back. So while things went pear-shaped with Amanda the girl way back, the ukulele has always kept the name – “Amanda” just seems to fit her really well.

And when I met Amanda Palmer & Neil Gaiman at the 2012 Edinburgh Fringe, there was only one thing to do…

I was supposed to know who Neil Gaiman was when Amanda signed this – I didn’t.

I don’t play her now as much as I used to – for a while there I was playing for an hour every day, but now it’s probably a few times a week. When the feeling grabs me. Like a lot of things I often think I should practice more, or I should write more songs. But to be honest I’m pretty happy just having her with me – either on her stand in my room or wrapped up in her carry bag when I’m travelling. She’s always with me and ready to play when I feel like it, rather than being compelled to follow some sort of rehearsal schedule.

And I guess that’s what playing music is really about – having a relationship with it, rather than it doing it because you have to.
